Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Vader Protocol, reviewing its governance, token, liquidity, staking, bonding, oracle, and utility components.

The review covered the System Architecture, including user and administrator entry points, governance and timelock controls, VADER and USDV conversion, legacy and V2 pool and router flows, synthetic assets, liquidity-provider tokens, staking rewards, bond treasury interactions, and external ERC-20 and oracle interfaces.

Our team confirmed two high-severity issues: ignored liquidity slippage parameters in router liquidity additions and donation-based XVader share-price manipulation. These issues can cause unfavorable liquidity positions or direct loss of deposited VADER. The protocol contains several access-control, pause, deadline, and reentrancy mechanisms, but the identified accounting and slippage weaknesses require remediation before the affected functionality can be considered secure.

System Overview

The Vader Protocol is organized around governance, core assets, decentralized exchange components, staking, bonding, and shared mathematical utilities. GovernorAlpha interacts with Timelock for proposal execution and administration, while XVader provides a governance-related share token. Owned, Pausable, RewardsDistributionRecipient, and StakingRewards establish administrative control, emergency pausing, reward distribution, and user staking flows.

VADER and USDV form the core token and conversion layer. The Vader token interacts with Converter and LinearVesting, while USDV uses LiquidityBasedTWAP for valuation-related decisions. VaderReserve also depends on the liquidity-based oracle and transfers reserve assets to users. External ERC-20 assets, Chainlink aggregator interfaces, UniswapV2Pair, and UniswapV2OracleLibrary cross the protocol boundary and introduce dependency and data-integrity considerations.

The trading and liquidity layer contains both legacy and V2 paths. VaderPoolFactory creates legacy VaderPool instances, and VaderRouter forwards user liquidity and trading operations to those pools while relying on VaderMath for calculations. VaderRouterV2 calls VaderPoolV2, which can create Synth assets through SynthFactory, mint synthetic tokens, and create LPWrapper and LPToken positions. Pool calculations depend on reserve state and shared mathematical utilities, making transaction ordering, rounding, and parameter propagation important security boundaries.

Our analysis revealed that legacy and V2 router liquidity paths expose minimum-amount parameters without enforcing them. The legacy path transfers the desired token amounts to the pool and allows reserve-sensitive liquidity calculations to determine the resulting position, so a trader can manipulate reserves before execution and cause a user to receive an economically unfavorable LP position. Deadline validation exists, but it does not replace minimum-output enforcement.

We also discovered that XVader derives share issuance and redemption from the contract's live VADER balance. Direct token donations can therefore alter the share price without issuing shares, and integer rounding can cause a later deposit to receive few or zero shares while existing holders redeem against the increased balance. The relevant components should use internally accounted assets or equivalent anti-donation controls, enforce minimum shares, and apply explicit slippage protections.

Legacy router ignores minimum amounts, enabling reserve-price sandwich attacks on liquidity additions

This is a logic error in the legacy router's slippage protection: the amountAMin and amountBMin parameters supplied to addLiquidity() are not enforced. As a result, the liquidity operation uses the pool's current spot reserves without validating the minimum amounts of each asset that the user expected to contribute. The absence of effective deadline validation further increases the window in which a stale transaction may execute under unfavorable conditions.

An attacker can monitor a pending liquidity-addition transaction, trade against the associated pool to manipulate its reserve ratio, and allow the victim's transaction to execute at the distorted price. The attacker can then trade back after the liquidity is added, potentially extracting value from the victim or causing the victim to receive materially less favorable liquidity exposure. The attack requires no privileged access or oracle compromise; ordinary pool trading combined with transaction ordering is sufficient.

Affected Components: the legacy_router (VaderRouter) contract, specifically the addLiquidity() function; the legacy_pool (VaderPool) contract, specifically its addLiquidity() function; and the vader_math library used for liquidity calculations

QA Review: Upon reviewing VaderRouter.sol:addLiquidity and BasePool.sol:mint, I confirmed that the core slippage vulnerability exists. The Uniswap-compatible router overload explicitly declares amountAMin and amountBMin but discards both values when forwarding to the six-argument addLiquidity function. That function applies only ensure(deadline), calls _addLiquidity, and _addLiquidity returns amountADesired and amountBDesired unconditionally rather than calculating amounts against current reserves or enforcing minimums. Both desired amounts are then transferred directly to the pool before pool.mint(to) executes. BasePool.mint derives liquidity from the current reserves and deposited balances through VaderMath.calculateLiquidityUnits, including a reserve-sensitive slip adjustment. Consequently, an attacker can trade against the pool before the victim transaction, alter the reserve ratio, and cause the victim’s fixed two-asset deposit to mint fewer liquidity units, effectively donating value to existing liquidity providers, before trading back. The deadline check is present and mitigates expiration, but it does not address reserve manipulation or minimum-output protection. VaderRouterV2 contains the same ignored-minimum pattern.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Enforce amountAMin and amountBMin against the actual amounts used by the pool, and add deadline validation. Ensure every router path that performs swaps or liquidity operations propagates and checks user slippage bounds before tokens are irrevocably transferred.

Direct VADER donations manipulate XVader share price and enable victim deposit extraction

This is an accounting and share-pricing logic error in XVader: share valuation is based on the contract's raw VADER token balance rather than on internally accounted assets. Because VADER can be transferred directly to the contract without going through the deposit mechanism, an external party can change the balance used for pricing and create donation-induced rounding distortions.

An attacker can acquire a small XVader share position, transfer VADER directly to the XVader contract to inflate the apparent asset balance and share price, and then wait for a victim to deposit. If the victim receives zero or materially fewer shares because of integer division, the attacker's existing shares may represent a disproportionate claim on the combined assets. The attacker can subsequently redeem those shares and extract value from the victim's deposit. This can result in direct asset loss for depositors and is particularly harmful for small deposits or systems with limited share-supply protections.

Affected Components: the XVader contract, specifically its VADER balance-based share-price calculation, deposit and mint-share path, and redemption and burn-share path; and the VADER token contract

QA Review: Upon reviewing the XVader contract, I confirmed that the donation-based share-price manipulation is exploitable. In enter(), the contract calculates totalVader using vader.balanceOf(address(this)) and derives new shares as (_amount * totalShares) / totalVader, while the VADER transfer occurs afterward. Because any holder can transfer VADER directly to XVader, an attacker can first acquire shares, donate VADER to increase the observed balance, and then front-run or precede a victim deposit. The victim may receive substantially fewer shares or zero shares due to integer division, with no minimum-share or zero-share check. In leave(), redemption is likewise based on the entire live VADER balance, so the attacker’s pre-existing shares can withdraw value attributable to the victim’s deposit. For example, with one initial share, a sufficiently large donation can make a subsequent victim deposit mint zero shares, after which the attacker can redeem the original share for the full balance. No internal asset accounting, donation exclusion, virtual liquidity, slippage protection, or minimum-mint requirement exists in the reviewed implementation.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use internally accounted assets for share pricing, or explicitly account for unsolicited donations. Add virtual shares/assets or equivalent anti-inflation protections, enforce minimum shares on deposits, and reject or safely handle deposits that would mint zero or economically negligible shares.

    uint256 totalVader = vader.balanceOf(address(this));
    // Gets the amount of xVader in existence
    uint256 totalShares = totalSupply();

        // vader deposited + gained from fees / withdrawn.

}

Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Abracadabra Money protocol, reviewing its lending, collateral custody, oracle, liquidity, rewards, Blast integrations, and deployment components.

Our review covered the system architecture, including DegenBox custody and share accounting; Cauldron borrowing, repayment, solvency, and liquidation flows; oracle pricing and exchange-rate updates; MIM and collateral interfaces; MagicLP pools, routers, factories, and aggregators; staking and rewards; Blast-specific onboarding and yield integrations; and deployment and testing tooling.

Our team identified four confirmed high-severity security concerns and one additional high-risk configuration concern. The principal risks involve unbounded collateralization parameters, instantaneous owner-controlled oracle replacement, fail-open use of stale exchange rates, and manipulation of spot-reserve MagicLP valuations. Deployment-time acceptance of arbitrary oracle configurations further increases the potential impact where deployment authority or configuration review is compromised. Overall, credit issuance and liquidation security depend heavily on trusted configuration, privileged oracle administration, and timely, manipulation-resistant pricing.

System Overview

The Hound team analyzed DegenBox as the protocol's custody and accounting layer. DegenBox maintains token balances, share totals, and strategy state, while withdrawal and share-conversion functions update this accounting. Transfer and flash-loan functionality can interact with IStrategy implementations. MasterContractManager is a privileged dependency, creating an important trust boundary around authorization and system configuration. CauldronV4 relies on the IBentoBoxV1 interface to hold collateral and borrowable assets and uses stored position, debt, accrual, and exchange-rate state to enforce lending rules.

Our analysis revealed that CauldronV4's borrow, repay, collateral-removal, cook, and liquidation paths are tightly coupled to oracle-derived exchange rates. The oracle update path stores a cached rate and downstream solvency calculations use that value. Liquidation also uses the exchange rate to determine collateral seizure and repayment amounts. CauldronV4 follows the ICauldronV2-to-ICauldronV3-to-ICauldronV4 interface hierarchy and uses CauldronLib for BentoBox-related accounting operations, while optional ISwapperV2 integrations can exchange collateral during liquidation.

We discovered that oracle trust is distributed across ProxyOracle, configured oracle implementations, and MagicLpAggregator. ProxyOracle delegates pricing to a replaceable implementation, while MagicLpAggregator derives MagicLP collateral value from current reserves, total supply, and underlying oracle answers. MagicLP reserves can change through permissionless swaps, and the reviewed architecture does not evidence sufficient time-weighting, deviation limits, liquidity checks, or independent validation. Consequently, oracle failures, privileged implementation replacement, or pool-state manipulation can directly affect borrowing capacity, solvency checks, and liquidation outcomes.

The Hound team also reviewed the MagicLP Router and Factory, fee-rate model contracts, LockingMultiRewards, and Blast-specific components including BlastTokenRegistry, BlastOnboardingData, BlastBox, BlastMagicLP, BlastCauldronV4, BlastGovernor, and BlastDapp. Blast yield and points libraries call external Blast interfaces and rebasing-token or points interfaces, introducing additional external trust boundaries. BlastGovernor includes owner-controlled arbitrary calls, yield claims, and configurable fee-recipient behavior, while onboarding components configure rewards, pools, registries, and related integrations.

Finally, we examined deployment and testing utilities such as CauldronDeployLib, BaseScript, Toolkit, and BaseTest. CauldronDeployLib passes deployment-selected oracle data and collateralization parameters into Cauldron initialization, making deployment validation a critical security control. The identified risks are concentrated in configuration invariants and oracle resilience: ltvBips lacks a demonstrated upper-bound check, stale exchange rates remain usable after oracle failure, ProxyOracle implementation changes are immediate, and spot MagicLP reserves can influence collateral valuation. These controls should be strengthened with strict parameter validation, timelocked and monitored oracle administration, freshness and deviation protections, and manipulation-resistant LP pricing.

Unbounded LTV configuration can create undercollateralized borrowing

This configuration-validation vulnerability allows an invalid loan-to-value (LTV) parameter to make collateral appear more valuable than intended. During deployment, the Cauldron deployment library encodes the caller-supplied ltvBips value without verifying that it is within the valid 0–10,000 bips range or below a protocol-approved maximum. Because the resulting parameter is used in borrowing and liquidation solvency calculations, a value above 10,000 bips can permit borrowing against materially insufficient collateral.

An attacker could exploit this issue if deployment configuration is attacker-influenced or if governance fails to validate the parameters before activation. The resulting undercollateralized positions could be converted into losses through BentoBox borrowing, while liquidation would be unable to recover the full outstanding debt. The issue should be addressed by enforcing both the numeric range and protocol-specific LTV limits before deployment and by validating the active collateralization parameters before enabling the Cauldron.

Affected Components: the CauldronDeployLib deployment library, specifically getCauldronParameters(), and the Cauldron contract, specifically its borrow/repay and liquidate functions and collateralized position state

QA Review: Upon reviewing CauldronDeployLib.getCauldronParameters and CauldronV4.init, I confirmed that the deployment path accepts an out-of-range LTV value without validation. The helper encodes ltvBips * 1e1 directly as COLLATERIZATION_RATE, while neither getCauldronParameters nor init enforces the documented 0-10,000 bips range or a protocol-specific maximum. CauldronV4._isSolvent then multiplies collateral value by COLLATERIZATION_RATE / 1e5, so a supplied value such as 20,000 bips produces a 200% collateralization rate and permits borrowing up to twice the collateral value, subject only to available MIM and borrow limits. The borrow and cook borrow paths invoke solvency checks using this inflated parameter, and liquidation uses the same solvency calculation, meaning positions can remain apparently solvent despite being economically undercollateralized. Although exploitation requires an untrusted deployment caller or inadequate governance review, the code provides no technical guard against that configuration error. The owner can also set the rate without bounds through setCollateralizationRate, reinforcing the missing invariant.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require ltvBips <= 10_000 and enforce protocol-specific maximum LTV bounds before encoding parameters; validate the resulting Cauldron collateralization rate after deployment and block activation when it exceeds approved limits.

Owner-controlled ProxyOracle implementation enables instant price substitution

This access-control and oracle price manipulation vulnerability allows a privileged ProxyOracle owner to replace the entire price implementation without a timelock, independent validation, or delayed activation. A compromised or abusive owner could install an implementation that returns arbitrary prices, including inflated collateral values or depressed values for liquidation purposes. The downstream Cauldron logic relies on the oracle result for solvency checks and collateral valuation.

After replacing the implementation, the privileged party could borrow against overvalued collateral or trigger liquidations using artificially depressed prices before governance or users can respond. The risk is amplified because the supplied architecture does not indicate cached-price sanity bounds, cross-oracle validation, or a mandatory delay for material implementation changes. Implementation updates should therefore be protected by multisignature governance and a timelock, while replacement contracts should be validated and downstream price-deviation circuit breakers should be enforced.

Affected Components: the ProxyOracle contract and the Cauldron contract, specifically the oracle rate update and liquidate functions

QA Review: Upon reviewing the ProxyOracle implementation and CauldronV4 oracle-dependent paths, I confirmed the privileged oracle-substitution risk. ProxyOracle.changeOracleImplementation() is callable by onlyOwner, but performs an immediate assignment of oracleImplementation without a timelock, validation of contract code or interface behavior, price continuity check, or independent approval. All subsequent decimals(), get(), peek(), and peekSpot() calls delegate directly to the replacement. CauldronV4.init(), updateExchangeRate(), and liquidate() consume oracle.get(oracleData), while borrow(), removeCollateral(), and cook() ultimately enforce solvency using the returned exchange rate through _isSolvent(). Consequently, a compromised or malicious ProxyOracle owner can install an oracle that returns arbitrary successful rates and influence borrowing, solvency checks, and liquidation calculations immediately. The cook rate bounds are caller-supplied transaction constraints, not an independent safeguard against a malicious implementation. This is a privileged-governance attack rather than a permissionless exploit, but the source clearly lacks the proposed defense mechanisms and exposes downstream protocol accounting to instantaneous owner-controlled oracle replacement.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Protect implementation changes with a timelock and multisignature governance, emit and monitor implementation-change events, require the replacement to satisfy an oracle interface and nonzero contract-code checks, and add downstream deviation bounds, circuit breakers, and delayed activation for materially different prices.

Oracle failure preserves stale exchange rates for borrowing and liquidation

This oracle price manipulation vulnerability results from fail-open handling of oracle update failures, whereby the previously cached exchange rate remains authoritative when a new rate cannot be obtained or validated. The cached rate is used by the Cauldron for collateral valuation, borrowing and solvency checks, and liquidation calculations. Consequently, the protocol may continue making credit decisions using a price that no longer reflects current market conditions.

An attacker could disrupt or manipulate the configured price source, submit data that causes an update to fail, or take advantage of market movements during oracle unavailability. If the stale rate overvalues collateral, the attacker could borrow more than the collateral supports; if it undervalues collateral, liquidation may be delayed or executed at an inaccurate price. The protocol should fail closed or enforce a maximum cache age, deviation limits, and suspension of credit-sensitive operations until a fresh, independently validated exchange rate is available.

Affected Components: the Cauldron contract, specifically the oracle rate update, borrow/repay, and liquidate functions, together with the cached exchange-rate state used for collateralized positions

QA Review: Upon reviewing CauldronV4.sol, I confirmed the stale-oracle vulnerability in updateExchangeRate, borrow, the solvent modifier, cook, and liquidate. updateExchangeRate calls oracle.get(oracleData) and, when updated is false, explicitly returns the previously stored exchangeRate without recording an update timestamp, enforcing a maximum age, or applying deviation bounds. The solvent modifier used by borrow and removeCollateral invokes this function and passes the returned rate to _isSolvent, so borrowing and collateral-withdrawal decisions remain based on stale data whenever the oracle fails. The cook path has the same behavior: ACTION_UPDATE_EXCHANGE_RATE only requires a fresh result when must_update is true, which callers can set to false. Most importantly, liquidate explicitly documents that the oracle may fail and uses the cached rate for both solvency checks and collateral seizure calculations. Thus, a market move during oracle outage or a source failure can preserve an overvalued collateral price, enabling undercollateralized borrowing and preventing timely liquidation. No freshness or fail-closed guard exists in the reviewed implementation.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Fail closed when an exchange-rate update fails, or enforce a maximum rate age and deviation bounds before allowing borrowing or liquidation. Consider pausing credit-sensitive operations until a fresh, independently validated price is available.

Spot-reserve MagicLP aggregator price can be manipulated before Cauldron valuation

This oracle price manipulation vulnerability arises because the MagicLP aggregator derives the LP valuation from instantaneous pool reserves and total supply without demonstrated time weighting, liquidity checks, deviation limits, or independent price validation. MagicLP swaps can modify the pool's PMM pricing state and reserves, allowing the aggregator's reported value to depend directly on short-term, permissionless pool activity.

An attacker could temporarily alter the pool reserves through a large swap, cause the Cauldron to refresh its exchange rate or perform a borrow or liquidation using the manipulated value, and then unwind the trade. Depending on pool liquidity, underlying-asset pricing, and deployment safeguards, this could enable excessive borrowing against inflated collateral or improperly priced liquidation. The aggregator should use a time-weighted and manipulation-resistant valuation, validate underlying prices and pool state against independent references, and reject material reserve or price deviations.

Affected Components: the MagicLpAggregator contract, the MagicLP contract, and the Aggregator interface

QA Review: Upon reviewing MagicLpAggregator.latestAnswer(), MagicLP.getReserves(), the permissionless sellBase() and sellQuote() functions, and CauldronV4.updateExchangeRate(), borrow(), cook(), and liquidate(), I confirmed a spot-reserve oracle-manipulation vulnerability. The aggregator obtains the current base and quote reserves, normalizes them, sums them, multiplies the result by the minimum of the two underlying oracle answers, and divides by pair.totalSupply(). It does not use MagicLP's cumulative price state, a TWAP, an independent LP valuation, liquidity thresholds, deviation limits, or a bounded rate-change check. MagicLP swaps update _BASE_RESERVE_ and _QUOTE_RESERVE_ through _setReserve(), so an attacker can trade against the pool to materially alter the valuation inputs while totalSupply remains unchanged. The attacker can then call CauldronV4.updateExchangeRate() or combine rate updating with borrowing through cook(), causing collateral solvency calculations to use the manipulated rate, and potentially unwind afterward. ProxyOracle only delegates calls and adds no protection. Although profitability depends on pool liquidity, fees, collateral balances, and underlying oracle behavior, the code provides a clear manipulation path and no effective mitigation.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use a time-weighted and manipulation-resistant valuation for MagicLP, validate both underlying asset prices and pool state, impose bounded rate-change limits and liquidity/deviation checks, and reject oracle updates when reserve composition or price deviates materially from an independent reference.

Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Dopex rDPX V2 protocol, focusing on its core contracts, perpetual vaults, liquidity-management components, oracle integrations, pricing libraries, and external decentralized-exchange interfaces.

Our review covered the system architecture, including asset and control flows between RdpxV2Core, the Perpetual Atlantic Vault and its LP contract, decaying bonds, reserves, ReLPContract, UniV2LiquidityAmo, UniV3LiquidityAmo, DpxEthOracle, option-pricing components, and Uniswap and StableSwap integrations.

The protocol has clear role-based controls and modular interfaces, but our analysis identified material price-manipulation exposure in the UniV2 and UniV3 AMO swap paths because execution depends on pool spot prices and caller-provided slippage parameters. We also identified elevated risks around oracle validation and LP share valuation that warrant additional safeguards. The overall security posture is functional but requires stronger independent price validation, deviation controls, and conservative handling of oracle-dependent operations.

System Overview

The Hound team analyzed Dopex rDPX V2 as a modular protocol centered on RdpxV2Core. The core coordinates interactions with the Perpetual Atlantic Vault, IRdpxDecayingBonds, RdpxReserve, IDpxEthToken, and liquidity-management contracts. It also provides funding to the perpetual vault, mints dpxETH and bond-related assets, and integrates with external StableSwap and Curve factory interfaces.

The PerpetualAtlanticVault uses IOptionPricing and IVolatilityOracle interfaces together with asset-price data supplied through IRdpxEthOracle and IDpxEthOracle. OptionPricingSimple delegates calculations to BlackScholes, which consumes asset-price and volatility data. These values feed option purchase, settlement, and funding flows involving the vault and PerpetualAtlanticVaultLP. The vault LP component calculates share issuance and redemption against vault collateral and underlying-price information, making oracle integrity important to both option settlement and accounting.

Our analysis revealed that ReLPContract coordinates rebalancing with IUniV2LiquidityAmo, IRdpxReserve, and IRdpxV2Core. UniV2LiquidityAmo transfers assets from the core, approves the Uniswap V2 router, and executes swaps against pair reserves using minimum-output parameters. UniV3LiquidityAmo similarly interacts with ISwapRouter, INonfungiblePositionManager, IUniswapV3Pool, and IUniswapV3Factory to manage concentrated-liquidity positions and execute swaps. Administrative authorization limits who can initiate these operations, but it does not prevent public observers from manipulating pool prices before a predictable transaction.

We discovered that the AMO swap paths lack an evident independent oracle or TWAP-based execution-price check, allowing permissive slippage settings to expose protocol assets to adverse spot-price execution and sandwiching. The architecture also relies on DpxEthOracle price publication and heartbeat validation, while the available design does not demonstrate robust deviation bounds, multi-source aggregation, or circuit breakers. Because PerpetualAtlanticVaultLP uses vault underlying-price data for share conversion, inaccurate or manipulated valuations could affect issuance and redemption fairness. Recommended protections include independent reference pricing, bounded deviations, short execution deadlines, strict economic limits, and conservative pauses for abnormal oracle or pool conditions.

UniV3 AMO swaps rely on manipulable pool spot price and caller-supplied slippage

This price-manipulation vulnerability arises because AMO swaps use the instantaneous Uniswap V3 pool price, while the minimum output and price limit are supplied by the caller without validation against an independent reference price. The AMO transfers assets from core, approves the Uniswap V3 router, and invokes exactInputSingle() using caller-controlled amountOutMinimum and sqrtPriceLimitX96 values. Although execution is restricted to DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE, that authorization does not prevent an attacker from manipulating the underlying pool price or sandwiching a predictable administrative transaction.

An attacker can monitor or predict an administrative AMO swap, execute a large trade to move the target pool price, and allow the AMO transaction to execute at the manipulated rate if its caller-supplied protections permit the trade. The attacker can then reverse the price movement after the transaction, potentially extracting value from the AMO and causing losses to the protocol's liquidity reserves. The AMO should derive execution bounds from an independent oracle or sufficiently long Uniswap V3 TWAP, reject materially stale or divergent prices, tightly calculate slippage limits, and consider private transaction submission for large swaps.

Affected Components: the UniV3LiquidityAmo contract, specifically the swap() function, together with the Uniswap V3 router interface's exactInputSingle() function and the Uniswap V3 pool interface

QA Review: Upon reviewing UniV3LiquidityAmo.swap in contracts/amo/UniV3LiquidityAmo.sol, I confirmed that the alleged spot-price manipulation risk exists. The function transfers funds from rdpxV2Core and calls univ3_router.exactInputSingle with _amountOutMinimum and _sqrtPriceLimitX96 copied directly from the administrator's call. No oracle, TWAP, deviation check, pool observation validation, or contract-enforced slippage bound is applied before execution. Although only DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE may invoke swap, that authorization does not prevent permissionless traders from observing and sandwiching the administrator's pending transaction. If the administrator supplies a permissive minimum output or price limit, an attacker can move the selected Uniswap V3 pool price immediately before execution, cause the AMO to trade at the manipulated spot price, and potentially reverse the trade afterward. Uniswap's router only enforces the caller-provided parameters; it does not establish fair value. The hardcoded deadline of 2105300114 also provides an unusually long execution window. Exploitability depends on transaction visibility, pool liquidity, and parameter choices, but the code clearly lacks an independent price safeguard.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Derive and enforce execution bounds from an independent oracle or sufficiently long Uniswap V3 TWAP, reject materially stale or divergent pool prices, use tightly calculated slippage limits rather than caller-selected permissive values, and consider private transaction submission for large swaps.

UniV2 AMO swaps can execute against attacker-manipulated pair spot prices

This price-manipulation vulnerability arises because UniV2 AMO swaps rely on manipulable Uniswap V2 pair reserves for execution pricing, with protection limited to a caller- or configuration-supplied minimum output. No independent oracle or fair-price validation is evidenced, so an authorized swap can execute at a materially distorted rate even when it satisfies the configured minimum-output constraint. Admin-only authorization limits who can initiate the swap but does not protect the transaction from reserve manipulation or front-running.

An attacker can monitor or predict an authorized AMO swap, temporarily trade against the relevant pair to skew its reserves, and allow the AMO transaction to execute at the manipulated price. The attacker can then reverse the reserve distortion and capture value from the AMO, potentially reducing protocol liquidity or transferring assets at an unfavorable exchange rate. The execution path should enforce price bounds derived from an independent oracle or sufficiently long TWAP, apply both minimum-output and maximum-input limits, use short transaction deadlines, and reject trades following excessive reserve or liquidity movements. Private transaction submission should also be considered for predictable large swaps.

Affected Components: the UniV2LiquidityAmo contract and its liquidity-manager asset flow, together with the Uniswap V2 router interface, pair interface, and reserve-pricing library

QA Review: Upon reviewing UniV2LiquidityAMO.swap, I confirmed that the AMO executes an exact-input Uniswap V2 trade using the router’s reserve-derived spot price without any independent fair-price validation. The function transfers token1 from rdpxV2Core, approves the configured router, constructs a two-token path, and calls swapExactTokensForTokens with only the caller-provided token2AmountOutMin and a one-second deadline. The router and UniswapV2Library calculate output from current pair reserves through getReserves and getAmountOut; the AMO does not consult addresses.rdpxOracle or impose a maximum input, price deviation, liquidity, or reserve-movement bound. An attacker able to observe a predictable administrator transaction can trade against the pair immediately before it, causing the AMO to receive materially less output while still satisfying a permissive amountOutMin, and then unwind the position afterward. Admin-only access limits who can initiate swaps but does not prevent public-mempool reserve manipulation or sandwiching. The short deadline reduces stale execution risk but does not provide price protection. Therefore, this is a real reserve-price manipulation exposure, although practical impact depends on transaction visibility and configured minimums.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Derive an execution price bound from an independent trusted oracle or sufficiently long TWAP, enforce both minimum-output and maximum-input constraints against that bound, use short deadlines and transaction-private submission where possible, and reject trades when pool liquidity or reserve movement exceeds configured limits.

Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the BlueBerry Protocol, examining its principal contracts, integrations, asset flows, oracle dependencies, vaults, wrappers, spells, and administrative configuration boundaries.

The review covered the system architecture, including how users and integrations enter through BlueBerryBank and vault interfaces; how spells coordinate leveraged positions; how SoftVault and HardVault manage assets; how wrappers custody external Curve, Convex, Aura, and ICHI positions; and how oracle modules, protocol configuration, and fee management support valuation and execution.

Our team identified several high-severity issues involving missing slippage protection and caller-controlled swap routes in CurveSpell, AuraSpell, and the Paraswap withdrawal path, as well as manipulable spot-price and Compound exchange-rate valuation paths. These issues can cause material asset loss, inaccurate collateral valuation, excessive borrowing, or bad debt. Additional oracle and collateral risks remain configuration-dependent or require further deployment-specific validation. The overall security posture should be considered high risk until the confirmed issues are remediated and the proposed oracle safeguards are independently validated.

System Overview

The Hound team analyzed BlueBerryBank as the central lending and position-management component. It implements IBank, coordinates SoftVault and HardVault operations, consults CoreOracle for token and collateral valuation, and interacts with FeeManager for fee accounting. Spells such as BasicSpell, CurveSpell, ConvexSpell, AuraSpell, IchiSpell, and ShortLongSpell route user-controlled position actions through the bank, wrappers, external protocols, and swap integrations.

SoftVault integrates with Compound-style ICErc20 markets and values deposited positions using the external market exchange rate. HardVault manages ERC1155-style position shares and uses ProtocolConfig and FeeManager for configuration and withdrawal-fee behavior. Wrapper contracts including WERC20, WCurveGauge, WConvexPools, WAuraPools, and WIchiFarm custody or tokenize external LP, gauge, farm, and reward positions while transferring underlying and reward assets across protocol boundaries.

Our analysis revealed that the oracle architecture is highly interconnected. CoreOracle routes valuation requests to AggregatorOracle and specialized sources including CurveOracle, BalancerPairOracle, UniswapV2Oracle, IchiVaultOracle, ChainlinkAdapterOracle, BandAdapterOracle, and UniswapV3AdapterOracle. Administrative controls, source configuration, time-gap limits, and deviation checks provide some safeguards, but reserve-based Uniswap V2 pricing and balance-derived Balancer pricing can remain vulnerable when configured without independent, manipulation-resistant references or a sufficient source quorum.

We discovered multiple execution paths that accept caller-controlled routers, token paths, or Paraswap calldata without effective local minimum-output enforcement. CurveSpell.closePositionFarm and AuraSpell.closePositionFarm perform reward liquidation with zero minimum output, while the ShortLongSpell withdrawal path forwards caller-selected Paraswap amounts without independently validating the received value. These boundaries expose harvested rewards and position assets to adverse execution, sandwiching, illiquid routes, and manipulated market prices.

The architecture also exposes collateral valuation dependencies that require particular attention. SoftVault collateral valuation relies on the Compound market's stored exchange rate without demonstrated freshness, deviation, or same-transaction protections, creating a potential over-borrowing path if the rate is manipulated. Curve and Convex valuation concerns are more dependent on pool implementations and deployment configuration, while the Uniswap V3 adapter uses historical observations rather than an immediate spot read. Remediation should prioritize bounded swap execution, approved routers and paths, independent oracle validation, conservative collateral valuation, and deployment-level testing of every supported external market.

Zero-slippage reward liquidation enables sandwich-based price manipulation

This is a slippage-control logic error in the reward liquidation flow. CurveSpell.closePositionFarm() invokes swapExactTokensForTokens() with the entire harvested reward amount while setting amountOutMin to zero. The router and swap path are also supplied by the caller, and the function does not appear to enforce an approved router, validate the token route, or compare the execution price with an independent reference price.

An attacker can monitor a closePositionFarm transaction, manipulate the relevant Uniswap V2 pool, or provide an adverse route that executes at an unfavorable price. Because the swap accepts any non-reverting output, the position can liquidate rewards for substantially less debt-token value than intended. The attacker can then reverse the temporary price manipulation and capture the resulting value loss, potentially impairing user positions and protocol solvency.

Affected Components: the CurveSpell contract, specifically the closePositionFarm() function, together with the Uniswap V2 router interface and the external Uniswap V2 deployment

QA Review: Upon reviewing CurveSpell.closePositionFarm in blueberry-core/contracts/spell/CurveSpell.sol, I confirmed the zero-slippage reward liquidation vulnerability. After harvesting rewards through _doCutRewardsFee(CRV), the function approves the caller-supplied swapRouter and invokes swapExactTokensForTokens with the entire reward balance, amountOutMin set to 0, the caller-supplied swapPath, and no oracle or execution-price validation. Consequently, an attacker can manipulate the relevant Uniswap V2 pool before this transaction executes, causing the reward-to-debt-token swap to receive materially less value, then reverse the manipulation afterward. The function also does not validate that the router, path endpoints, intermediate tokens, or liquidity sources are protocol-approved. Although subsequent Curve removal and repayment logic may cause the transaction to revert if insufficient debt tokens are received, a successful adverse swap can still impose value loss where the remaining flow succeeds; moreover, arbitrary routers introduce additional trust and token-transfer risks. The supplied oracle contracts do not protect this swap, as neither is consulted by closePositionFarm.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require a caller-specified minimum output constrained by a protocol-configured maximum slippage, validate the router and token path against an allowlist, and preferably compare the execution price with a manipulation-resistant oracle or TWAP before accepting the swap.

PSwapLib forwards caller-controlled Paraswap slippage without oracle or minimum-output enforcement

This is an input-validation and slippage-protection logic flaw in the Paraswap integration. PSwapLib.megaSwap() forwards caller-controlled MegaSwapSellData directly to Paraswap without independently validating the minimum destination amount, expected execution price, or permitted route. In particular, a caller may be able to provide a near-zero toAmount, subject to any validation performed by the ShortLongSpell entry point.

An attacker or malicious caller can submit swap data that causes assets to be exchanged at a materially unfavorable price, including during temporary market manipulation. The resulting loss may reduce the value returned from a withdrawal or distort the economics of a leveraged strategy. The integration should independently constrain toAmount and permitted assets and routes rather than relying solely on calldata supplied to Paraswap.

Affected Components: the PSwapLib library, specifically the megaSwap() function; the Utils library, specifically the MegaSwapSellData structure; the ShortLongSpell contract, specifically the _withdraw() function; and the external Paraswap deployment

QA Review: Upon reviewing PSwapLib.megaSwap and ShortLongSpell.closePosition/_withdraw, I confirmed that the spell performs no independent slippage or minimum-output validation for withdrawals. closePosition only verifies that swapData.fromToken matches the strategy’s underlying token, then passes the entire caller-controlled MegaSwapSellData to _withdraw. _withdraw checks param.sellSlippage against the configured maximum, but never relates that value to swapData.toAmount, expectedAmount, the withdrawn amount, or an oracle-derived valuation. PSwapLib merely approves fromAmount and invokes the unprotected IParaswap.megaSwap entry point. Although Paraswap may enforce the supplied toAmount as a minimum at execution, that minimum itself is selected by the caller and can be set near zero; expectedAmount is not checked during withdrawal, unlike _deposit, which explicitly compares the received balance against swapData.expectedAmount. Consequently, a caller can authorize a materially underpriced route or near-zero output, and the protocol has no local protection against it. The precise impact and whether an attacker can profit depend on caller authorization and broader position-management logic, but the stated missing validation is present.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Validate swapData.toAmount against an independently obtained oracle or strategy quote and enforce a bounded maximum slippage before calling Paraswap. Reject zero or insufficient minimum outputs, constrain allowed routes and tokens, and prefer the protected Paraswap entry point where its beneficiary and execution protections are applicable.

AggregatorOracle can return flash-loan-manipulated Uniswap V2 spot prices

This is an oracle-design vulnerability caused by reliance on manipulable Uniswap V2 spot prices. AggregatorOracle permits configured price sources that may use reserve-based Uniswap V2 pricing without requiring a time-weighted average price, an independent reference, or sufficient source independence. Deviation checks alone do not prevent manipulation when a single source, or multiple correlated sources, are affected by the same pool movement.

An attacker can temporarily trade against the underlying Uniswap V2 pool before a financially sensitive operation queries AggregatorOracle, causing the reported asset price to move in the attacker’s favor. The attacker can then reverse the trade after the operation completes. If the oracle is used for collateral valuation, borrowing, liquidation, or settlement, the manipulated price may enable undercollateralized borrowing or cause assets to be transferred at an incorrect value.

Affected Components: the AggregatorOracle contract and its configured route aggregation and deviation-validation logic, the UniswapV2Oracle contract and its reserve-based price calculation, the downstream oracle routing in the oracle core, and the external Uniswap V2 deployment

QA Review: Upon reviewing AggregatorOracle.getPrice, UniswapV2Oracle.getPrice, and CoreOracle._getPrice, I confirmed that the implementation permits a manipulable reserve-based oracle path. UniswapV2Oracle.getPrice reads pool.getReserves() directly and uses those current reserves in its fair LP pricing formula, without any TWAP, observation-period, liquidity-depth, or freshness validation. AggregatorOracle._setPrimarySources permits up to three arbitrary sources, does not require a minimum source count, does not verify source independence, and getPrice explicitly returns a single valid source without applying deviation checks. Consequently, if an administrator configures UniswapV2Oracle as the sole source, or configures correlated sources, a transaction that changes the pair state before the oracle call can cause the aggregator to return a transient valuation; CoreOracle then accepts any nonzero result from that route. The deviation logic only compares returned values and cannot detect synchronized manipulation or protect a single-source configuration. The supplied code does not show actual deployment routes or downstream liquidation logic, but the exploitable oracle design and missing safeguards are clearly present.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Disallow spot-reserve routes for collateral valuation, or require a sufficiently long TWAP and minimum-liquidity/depth checks. Require multiple independent sources, enforce a minimum source count, reject duplicate or correlated routes, and fail closed when sources deviate beyond the configured bound.

SoftVault Compound exchange-rate inflation can overvalue collateral

This is a collateral-valuation and oracle-integrity vulnerability involving externally mutable Compound exchange rates. SoftVault appears to rely on the Compound market’s exchange rate when valuing shares or deposited assets, while no effective rate bounds, freshness checks, time-weighted accounting, donation resistance, or same-transaction manipulation protection are evident for this path. BlueBerryBank can then use the resulting SoftVault position as collateral.

An attacker may acquire or flash-borrow underlying assets and supply or donate them to the Compound market to increase its exchange rate. If the inflated rate is used during a deposit or collateral valuation, the attacker may obtain excess borrowing capacity from BlueBerryBank against overvalued collateral. After the market rate normalizes, the position may become undercollateralized, leaving the bank and its depositors exposed to losses.

Affected Components: the SoftVault contract and its deposit and share-valuation logic, the external Compound market and its exchange-rate accounting, and the BlueBerryBank contract and its collateral-valuation and borrowing logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing SoftVault.deposit, BlueBerryBank.lend, and BlueBerryBank.getIsolatedCollateralValue, I confirmed a spot exchange-rate manipulation vulnerability in the collateral valuation path. SoftVault issues shares equal to the cToken balance increase returned by Compound mint, while getIsolatedCollateralValue converts those shares to underlying using ICErc20(bToken).exchangeRateStored() multiplied by pos.underlyingVaultShare and divided by PRICE_PRECISION. This value is then passed directly to oracle.getTokenValue without any bound, freshness, historical-rate, deviation, or same-transaction protection. An attacker can acquire SoftVault shares, manipulate the Compound market’s cash balance and exchange rate by supplying or directly transferring underlying to the cToken contract, and then invoke a borrowing path that relies on the inflated isolated collateral value. The bank’s only relevant control is the liquidation-threshold check in execute, which itself uses the same manipulated valuation and therefore does not prevent over-borrowing. Although exact profitability depends on market liquidity, share ownership, and Compound implementation details, the code clearly exposes collateral valuation to a mutable spot exchange rate and provides no mitigation in the reviewed components.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use a conservative, bounded Compound exchange-rate adapter; base collateral value on a historical or independently verified rate, cap per-block changes, apply deviation checks, and prevent same-transaction deposits or valuation from using an attacker-controlled rate update.

Aura reward liquidation may use manipulable Uniswap spot pricing without an effective minimum-output bound

This is a reward-liquidation pricing and slippage-control vulnerability in the Aura integration. The Aura reward-conversion path is connected to an external Uniswap market, but it does not demonstrate effective validation against a manipulation-resistant oracle, a time-weighted price, or a strictly enforced minimum-output amount. Consequently, the amount received for harvested rewards may depend on an attacker-influenceable single-block market price.

An attacker can manipulate the relevant Uniswap pool immediately before an Aura position is burned or harvested, causing AuraSpell to liquidate rewards at a distorted rate. The attacker can then unwind the manipulation after the liquidation and capture excess value, or cause the protocol or position owner to receive materially fewer underlying assets than expected. This may reduce user returns and create losses for positions that depend on the reward conversion.

Affected Components: the AuraSpell contract and its reward-liquidation flow, the external Uniswap deployment and relevant swap pool, and the WAuraPools wrapper contracts and their reward-claiming logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing AuraSpell.closePositionFarm and WAuraPools.burn, I confirmed that the reward liquidation path performs an unprotected Uniswap V2-style swap. After burning the position and receiving rewardTokens, closePositionFarm calls swapRouter.swapExactTokensForTokens(rewards, 0, swapPath[i], address(this), type(uint256).max), explicitly setting amountOutMin to zero and using an effectively unlimited deadline. The caller also supplies the router and each swap path, while no TWAP, oracle quote, price-impact limit, or deviation check is performed before or after execution. Consequently, an attacker able to influence the selected Uniswap pool can sandwich or otherwise distort reserves immediately before the transaction, causing rewards to be exchanged at an arbitrarily poor rate without reverting. WAuraPools.burn only calculates and transfers reward amounts; it provides no valuation or slippage protection. The UniswapV2Oracle shown is an LP pricing oracle and is not consulted by this liquidation path. Although the direct economic impact may depend on who invokes the close operation and the resulting debt accounting, the absence of any minimum-output bound and reliance on router spot execution clearly establish the reported vulnerability.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use a bounded minimum-output parameter derived from a trusted TWAP or Chainlink-style oracle, enforce a maximum price-impact/deviation check, and reject swaps whose execution price deviates from the reference price beyond a strict threshold. Avoid valuing harvested rewards using a single-block Uniswap spot quote.

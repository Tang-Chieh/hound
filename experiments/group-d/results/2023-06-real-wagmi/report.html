Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Wagmi Concentrator, reviewing its contract architecture, trust boundaries, asset flows, administrative controls, pricing logic, and interactions with external protocols.

The review covered the high-level system architecture, including the Factory, Multipool, MultiStrategy, and Dispatcher contracts; their interfaces, storage, deployment artifacts, mathematical libraries, emitted events, ERC20 flows, and dependencies on Uniswap V3 pools and approved swap targets.

Our team identified three confirmed security issues centered on the use of manipulable spot prices for liquidity-range construction, deposit valuation, and Dispatcher reserve-based accounting. These findings create material risks to liquidity placement, share issuance, fee attribution, and user withdrawals, with severity ranging from medium to high. We also verified that the rebalance swap path includes balance-delta and TWAP-based output validation, while the Factory spot quote is not used internally for settlement. The overall security posture requires remediation of spot-price-dependent accounting and position management before relying on the system for substantial value.

System Overview

The Wagmi Concentrator is organized around the Factory, Multipool, MultiStrategy, and Dispatcher contracts. Factory deploys the Dispatcher, Multipool, and MultiStrategy instances, initializes their relationships, records created pools, and registers pools with Dispatcher. Deployment relies on embedded bytecode artifacts and on the Uniswap V3 factory and pool interfaces to identify and interact with underlying liquidity pools.

Multipool is the primary asset-management component. It accepts ERC20 deposits, mints and burns pool shares, maintains trusted Uniswap V3 pool positions, collects fees, performs withdrawals, calculates reserves and quotes, and coordinates rebalancing. It uses FullMath, LiquidityAmounts, TickMath, Babylonian, and ErrLib for arithmetic, liquidity conversion, square-root, tick, and error-handling operations. Multipool can also authorize configured swap targets and transfer assets to and from trusted underlying pools.

MultiStrategy stores the active strategy and associated Multipool, allowing authorized management operations to change or apply strategy configuration. Dispatcher maintains pool and user accounting, obtains snapshots from Multipool, and performs deposit and withdrawal flows for users. These flows transfer ERC20 assets, calculate share and fee amounts from reported reserves and total supply, and emit events for pool creation, deposits, withdrawals, strategy changes, parameter updates, trusted-pool changes, and rebalancing activity.

Our analysis revealed that several security-sensitive calculations rely on instantaneous Uniswap V3 state. Multipool position ranges can be derived from the current slot0 tick during initialization and rebalancing, allowing temporary price distortion to influence where liquidity is reopened. Multipool deposits likewise use spot-derived slot data for reserve and liquidity calculations, which can affect the assets accepted and shares minted. Dispatcher snapshot-based accounting can also use manipulated reserve proportions when converting fee entitlements and processing withdrawal-related operations.

The system does contain meaningful controls: access restrictions limit management and operator actions, trusted pools and approved swap targets are explicitly configured, arithmetic libraries provide checked financial calculations, and the rebalance swap path compares realized balance deltas with a TWAP-derived output and deviation limit. However, those controls do not protect all spot-dependent paths. The Hound team recommends introducing validated TWAP or independent oracle checks for position ticks, deposit valuation, and Dispatcher accounting, together with explicit spot-to-TWAP deviation limits and conservative user-facing minimum-output protections.

Dispatcher reserve-based share pricing can be manipulated during deposits and withdrawals

This is a share-pricing and oracle-validation vulnerability in which the Dispatcher relies on instantaneous reserve-based valuations during deposits and withdrawals without visibly enforcing a manipulation-resistant oracle or a bounded spot-to-TWAP deviation. The Dispatcher obtains reserve balances, fee-growth data, and total supply through snapshot() and uses those values to calculate the amount of shares issued or assets returned. A configured MAX_DEVIATION value does not, by itself, demonstrate that the reserve valuation used for share conversion is protected against temporary price manipulation.

An attacker can execute a large swap or use flash liquidity to distort the underlying Uniswap V3 pool price, then call Dispatcher.deposit() or withdraw() while the manipulated reserve ratio is reflected in snapshot(). After reversing the price manipulation, the attacker may retain excess shares or assets obtained from the incorrect conversion. This can dilute existing shareholders or transfer value from the pool to the attacker. Share conversions should use a sufficiently long TWAP or independently validated oracle, enforce a maximum spot-to-reference deviation before conversion, and apply conservative minimum-output and maximum-input constraints to both deposits and withdrawals.

Affected Components: the Dispatcher contract, specifically the deposit and withdraw share-conversion logic; the Dispatcher pool-information storage used by snapshot(); and the Multipool contract, specifically snapshot() and its reserve-calculation logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing Dispatcher.deposit, Dispatcher.withdraw, Multipool.snapshot, and Multipool._getReserves, I confirmed that withdrawal-related share accounting can depend on a manipulable Uniswap V3 spot state. Dispatcher obtains reserves and total supply from snapshot(), then converts accrued token fees into LP shares through _estimateWithdrawalLp(), which averages fee0/reserve0 and fee1/reserve1. Multipool.snapshot first calls _earn(), withdrawing all active liquidity, and then derives reserves using getSlots() and current Uniswap positions; these values are not checked against a TWAP or an independent oracle. Consequently, an attacker can distort an underlying pool price, invoke deposit (which also claims existing fees) or partial withdraw during the distortion, and cause fee entitlements to be converted using manipulated reserve proportions before reversing the price. MAX_DEVIATION in Dispatcher is not an oracle bound: it only scales minimum-output amounts, while deviationBP is caller-supplied. Multipool's TWAP check is confined to operator rebalance logic and does not protect snapshot-based Dispatcher accounting. The issue is principally a withdrawal and fee-claim manipulation rather than minting new shares from a deposit.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use a manipulation-resistant TWAP or independently validated oracle for token conversion, enforce maximum deviation against the oracle before any share conversion, and apply conservative minimum-output and maximum-input checks to both deposits and withdrawals. Consider preventing same-transaction reserve-price changes from affecting share accounting.

Public Multipool deposits can mint shares against a transiently manipulated Uniswap V3 price

This is a price-oracle validation vulnerability in which public Multipool deposits calculate liquidity and mint LP shares using instantaneous Uniswap V3 slot0 values without visible TWAP or oracle validation. The deposit flow obtains current slot data through getSlots() and passes it to _deposit(), while the available checks only confirm conditions such as positive liquidity and compliance with the maximum total supply. They do not establish that the price used for share issuance is resistant to temporary manipulation or within an acceptable deviation from a trusted reference.

An attacker can perform a large temporary swap in an underlying trusted Uniswap V3 pool, submit a public deposit while slot0 is distorted, and provide the token composition that receives favorable treatment under the manipulated valuation. Once the price is restored, the attacker may withdraw the resulting position for more value than was deposited, causing losses or dilution for other pool participants. Deposits should use a sufficiently long TWAP or validated oracle, reject transactions when spot price deviates beyond a configured bound, and require user-specified minimum shares or maximum token amounts based on the protected valuation.

Affected Components: the Multipool contract, specifically the public deposit path, _deposit(), and getSlots() functions; the underlying Uniswap V3 pool; and the liquidity and tick-math libraries used to calculate deposit liquidity and shares

QA Review: Upon reviewing the Multipool deposit path, I confirmed that share issuance relies on manipulable Uniswap V3 spot prices. In deposit(), existing pools call getSlots(), which reads each trusted pool’s slot0() directly without consulting the configured twapDuration or maxTwapDeviation. Those values are passed to _getReserves(), where LiquidityAmounts.getAmountsForLiquidity() calculates the pool reserves at the manipulated spot, and to _optimizeAmounts(), which determines the token composition accepted from the depositor. The resulting lpAmount is then calculated from those spot-derived reserves before _deposit() mints liquidity using the same instantaneous sqrt price. The only relevant safeguards are user-supplied amount minimums, a positive-liquidity check, and maxTotalSupply; none validates the price or limits deviation from a time-weighted valuation. Although getAmountOut() uses a TWAP, that function is limited to rebalance slippage validation and is not used by deposit(). Therefore, an attacker can temporarily move an underlying pool price, deposit using the favorable spot-derived composition, restore the price, and potentially redeem disproportionately valuable shares.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Base deposit liquidity and share pricing on a sufficiently long TWAP or validated oracle, reject deposits when spot deviates beyond a configured bound, and require user-specified minimum shares or maximum token amounts based on the protected valuation.

medium

Spot-price manipulation can force rebalance liquidity into attacker-selected Uniswap V3 ranges

This is a price-oracle validation vulnerability in which Multipool position ranges are derived from an attacker-influenceable Uniswap V3 spot tick rather than from a manipulation-resistant observation. During strategy initialization and rebalancing, the contract reads slot0.tick to calculate the lower and upper ticks for liquidity positions. Although the rebalance path validates swap execution using getAmountOut and maxTwapDeviation, that validation does not constrain the separate spot tick used for position placement.

An attacker can temporarily manipulate the relevant underlying Uniswap V3 pool immediately before an authorized rebalance, causing the operator transaction to read a distorted tick and allocate liquidity to attacker-selected ranges. The attacker can then unwind the manipulation or arbitrage the resulting mispositioned liquidity. This may reduce capital efficiency, expose the pool to unfavorable price ranges, and cause losses through adverse liquidity placement. Position ranges should instead be derived from a sufficiently long TWAP or independently validated oracle, with the rebalance reverted when the spot price falls outside an approved deviation from that reference.

Affected Components: the Multipool contract, specifically the _initializeStrategy(), rebalanceAll(), and _getTicksForPosition() functions; the underlying Uniswap V3 pool used for pricing; and the authorized manager or operator responsible for executing rebalances

QA Review: Upon reviewing Multipool._initializeStrategy, _getTicksForPosition, and rebalanceAll, I confirmed that the hypothesized spot-price manipulation is possible. During initialization, each strategy position obtains the underlying pool's current tick directly from IUniswapV3Pool.slot0(), then passes that attacker-influenceable value to _getTicksForPosition to derive lowerTick and upperTick. rebalanceAll invokes _initializeStrategy only after withdrawing existing liquidity and executing any configured swap, so an attacker who temporarily moves an underlying pool price and preserves the distortion until the operator transaction executes can cause the new positions to be created around manipulated, attacker-selected ranges. The subsequent swap validation is not an effective defense: getAmountOut() uses a TWAP from the hardcoded fee-500 pool and only checks swap output, while no TWAP, spot/TWAP deviation, or independently supplied tick bound is applied to the ticks used for range construction. Tick spacing and absolute TickMath bounds only ensure syntactic validity, not price authenticity. Exploitability depends on transaction ordering and manipulation cost, but the code provides a clear exploitable path.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Derive rebalance position ticks from a sufficiently long TWAP or an oracle observation with a bounded spot/TWAP deviation, and revert when the current tick is outside the permitted range. Consider calculating and validating all price-sensitive rebalance parameters before execution and protecting the rebalance transaction from stale or manipulated observations.

    int24 tickSpacingOffset,
    int24 tickSpacing

        lowerTick = floorTick + tickSpacing * tickSpacingOffset;
        upperTick = lowerTick + positionRange;

        lowerTick = upperTick - positionRange;
    }
}

Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the MarginSwap protocol, reviewing its contract architecture, operational flows, asset custody, valuation logic, lending mechanisms, margin trading, liquidation, and incentive distribution components.

The audit scope covered the high-level system architecture, including external trader and token interactions, role and service discovery, router-mediated isolated- and cross-margin operations, lending and bond accounting, collateral custody through Fund, price conversion and liquidation, and incentive distribution.

Our team identified two confirmed high-severity issues affecting cross-margin liquidation. Liquidation valuations can rely on manipulable instantaneous AMM reserves, and liquidation swaps execute without meaningful slippage bounds. These weaknesses may allow reserve manipulation to trigger improper liquidations or cause protocol-controlled trades to execute at materially adverse prices, creating potential collateral losses and liquidation shortfalls. The overall security posture requires remediation of oracle integrity and liquidation execution controls before relying on these flows for robust asset protection.

System Overview

The Hound team analyzed MarginSwap as a modular Solidity protocol centered on MarginRouter, which coordinates user-facing trading and lending operations. RoleAware modules obtain privileged service addresses through Roles and cached role references, while Fund provides centralized custody and disbursement for ERC-20 assets and WETH. Admin manages protocol configuration and can interact with Fund and IncentiveDistribution. This service-discovery model reduces hard-coded dependencies but makes role registry integrity and privileged account management critical security boundaries.

MarginRouter routes operations into IsolatedMarginTrading and CrossMarginTrading. IsolatedMarginTrading builds on IsolatedMarginLiquidation and IsolatedMarginAccounts, maintaining per-position account records and invoking registration or unwind logic. CrossMarginTrading similarly extends CrossMarginLiquidation and CrossMarginAccounts, with account-level collateral and borrowing state shared across positions. Cross-margin operations can borrow from Lending, update account mappings, and use Fund for asset movement, while liquidation components can call the router, Fund, and Admin and record liquidation stake information.

Lending combines BaseLending, BondLending, and HourlyBondSubscriptionLending. It tracks yield through time-based accumulators and maintains bond and hourly-bond records, while depositing and withdrawing assets through Fund. IncentiveDistribution and IncentivizedHolder maintain tranche claims and accrued rewards, with reward withdrawals ultimately funded by Fund. These accounting and custody flows create important consistency requirements between recorded balances, accrued yield, token transfers, and privileged withdrawal paths.

PriceAware supplies valuation and conversion functions to the margin and liquidation components. It depends on UniswapStyleLib and the configured Uniswap V2 pair boundary for AMM-derived prices, while the protocol also maintains cached or EMA-style pricing information. Our analysis revealed that the liquidation path can force current-block valuation and thereby use instantaneous AMM reserve quotes rather than a manipulation-resistant cached price. The confirmed finding was identified by openai-codex/gpt-5.6-luna.

We discovered a related liquidation execution weakness: PriceAware liquidation helpers call MarginRouter swap functions with zero minimum output or an unbounded maximum input, and the router derives execution amounts from live AMM reserves. Consequently, an attacker can skew reserves before liquidation and cause protocol-controlled swaps to execute at unfavorable rates. This confirmed finding was identified by openai-codex/gpt-5.6-luna. Mitigation should combine protected oracle or TWAP pricing, deviation checks, conservative slippage limits, and transaction-size controls relative to pool liquidity.

Cross-margin liquidation valuation uses manipulable Uniswap spot reserves instead of the cached EMA

This oracle price manipulation vulnerability results from the cross-margin liquidation path using instantaneous Uniswap reserve data instead of the configured cached EMA or another manipulation-resistant price source. When the forceCurBlock path is enabled, the valuation functions obtain current AMM quotes for both collateral and debt assets, bypassing the update-window and EMA protections intended to limit short-term price manipulation.

An attacker can temporarily trade against the configured liquidation pair to inflate the debt-token price or suppress the collateral-token price, then submit liquidation candidates while the reserves remain distorted. This may cause solvent accounts to be liquidated and can also miscalculate repayment amounts, collateral valuations, and maintainer fees. Liquidation eligibility and all related payout calculations should use a validated cached or TWAP-based oracle price, with stale-price and independent deviation checks, rather than allowing forceCurBlock to bypass the protected pricing path.

Affected Components: the CrossMarginLiquidation and CrossMarginAccounts contracts, specifically liquidate(), holdingsInPeg(), and loanInPeg(), and the PriceAware contract, specifically getCurrentPriceInPeg(), viewCurrentPriceInPeg(), and getPriceFromAMM(), together with the configured Uniswap library and AMM pair

QA Review: Upon reviewing the liquidation flow in CrossMarginLiquidation.sol and the valuation logic in CrossMarginAccounts.sol and PriceAware.sol, I confirmed the oracle price manipulation vulnerability. calcLiquidationAmounts() invokes belowMaintenanceThreshold(), which calls loanInPeg(account, true) and holdingsInPeg(account, true); liquidate() then repeats both valuations with true before calculating repayments and maintainer fees. In PriceAware.getCurrentPriceInPeg(), forceCurBlock does not use the cached EMA: when the cache is recent it calls viewCurrentPriceInPeg(), which directly computes a Uniswap reserve quote, and when stale it calls getPriceFromAMM(), which also returns the instantaneous quote even if it updates the EMA. Thus both liquidation eligibility and settlement amounts depend on manipulable reserves from the configured liquidation path. An attacker able to temporarily skew that pair can make solvent accounts appear liquidatable or distort debt, collateral, and fee calculations, then unwind the trade after liquidation. The documented EMA and update-window protections do not guard this forceCurBlock path, and no deviation or TWAP validation is present.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use a manipulation-resistant oracle or a validated cached/TWAP price for liquidation eligibility, debt valuation, and payout calculations. Do not allow forceCurBlock to bypass the protected price path; add stale-price handling and independent deviation checks against AMM execution quotes.

Liquidation swaps execute against manipulable AMM reserves with no slippage bound

This liquidation execution price manipulation vulnerability results from protocol-controlled swaps relying on live AMM reserve quotes without enforcing slippage limits. The liquidation functions invoke the router with a minimum output of zero for liquidateToPeg and a maximum input of uint256 maximum for liquidateFromPeg. Although the router checks the amount received against the manipulated quote, it does not establish that the execution price is fair or consistent with an independent valuation.

An attacker can front-run or otherwise move the configured AMM pair before a liquidation transaction, causing the protocol to accept negligible peg-token proceeds or spend an excessive amount of peg tokens to acquire the required asset. The attacker can subsequently restore the pool price or trade against the protocol's adverse execution, creating losses for the protocol and potentially reducing liquidation proceeds available to creditors or maintainers. The protocol should derive minimum-output and maximum-input limits from an independent oracle or protected TWAP, enforce conservative deviation bounds, and limit liquidation trade sizes relative to available pool liquidity.

Affected Components: the PriceAware contract, specifically liquidateToPeg() and liquidateFromPeg(), and the MarginRouter contract, specifically authorizedSwapExactT4T(), authorizedSwapT4ExactT(), _swapExactT4T(), and _swapT4ExactT(), together with the configured Uniswap library, AMM pair, and protocol fund contract

QA Review: Upon reviewing CrossMarginLiquidation.liquidateToPeg and liquidateFromPeg, PriceAware.sol, and MarginRouter.sol, I confirmed the liquidation execution price manipulation vulnerability. liquidateToPeg calls authorizedSwapExactT4T with amountOutMin set to zero, while liquidateFromPeg calls authorizedSwapT4ExactT with amountInMax set to uint256.max. MarginRouter computes execution amounts directly from current Uniswap-style reserves through getAmountsOut or getAmountsIn, and its subsequent balance check only verifies that the AMM delivered the already-quoted amount; it does not compare execution against an independent valuation. The cached EMA-style price in PriceAware is used for valuation and borrowing logic, but is not used to derive liquidation slippage limits. Consequently, an attacker can trade against the configured liquidation pair before the liquidation transaction, skewing reserves so that the protocol accepts a poor output when selling collateral or pays excessive peg input when acquiring debt assets, then potentially reverse the trade afterward. noIntermediary and the EOA-oriented liquidation design do not prevent public mempool reserve manipulation.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Derive minimum-output and maximum-input limits from an independent oracle/TWAP with conservative deviation bounds, or use deep/liquidation-specific auctions instead of spot AMM execution. Revert when execution deviates materially from the protected valuation and cap protocol trade size relative to pool liquidity.

Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Abracadabra Money protocol and its Blast-specific integrations, with emphasis on custody, lending, liquidation, oracle pricing, swaps, staking, rewards, and deployment controls.

The review covered the high-level system architecture, including DegenBox custody and share accounting; Cauldron collateral, borrowing, repayment, solvency, and liquidation flows; oracle and MagicLP pricing; MIM and collateral interfaces; MagicLP routers and factories; staking and reward distribution; Blast onboarding, yield, and points integrations; and deployment and testing tooling.

Our team identified several material security and resilience concerns. The principal risks involve stale or invalid oracle rates being accepted by Cauldron solvency checks, malformed MagicLP aggregator inputs causing oracle-dependent operations to revert, staking and reward accounting for fee-on-transfer or rebasing tokens, and whitelist removal failing to revoke existing master-contract authority. Overall, the system contains strong modular controls and explicit authorization boundaries, but oracle validation, emergency revocation, and non-standard-token handling require remediation before the affected components can be considered robust.

System Overview

The Hound team analyzed DegenBox as the protocol's custody and share-accounting layer. DegenBox maintains token balances, totals, strategy state, and share conversions, while withdrawal and transfer flows interact with MasterContractManager authorization. Master contracts can operate on behalf of users through stored associations and approvals, creating a critical trust boundary around approval state and emergency revocation. Our analysis revealed that disabling a master contract in the whitelist does not clear existing associations or approvals, so previously authorized contracts may retain the ability to withdraw, transfer, or deposit for users. This finding was confirmed by openai-codex/gpt-5.6-luna.

CauldronV4 uses DegenBox for collateral custody and MIM borrowing, with position state tracking collateral shares, borrow parts, accrued interest, and exchange rates. Borrowing, repayment, collateral removal, liquidation, and cook operations are coupled to solvency checks and oracle updates. The oracle path caches the prior exchange rate when an update fails, without a freshness bound or borrowing restriction, and also accepts a successful zero rate without a protocol-level sanity check. These behaviors can permit risk-increasing actions against stale or invalid collateral valuations. The related findings were confirmed by openai-codex/gpt-5.6-luna.

The oracle architecture includes ProxyOracle and MagicLpAggregator, with MagicLP pool data and underlying aggregator answers feeding collateral valuation. We reviewed the data flow from external price sources through the aggregator into CauldronV4's exchange-rate state. The MagicLP aggregator does not adequately validate negative feed answers, excessive decimals, arithmetic bounds, or zero total supply; resulting reverts can propagate into borrowing, liquidation, collateral removal, and other oracle-dependent operations. This denial-of-service concern was confirmed by openai-codex/gpt-5.6-luna.

The system also includes MagicLP pools, Router and Factory components, fee-rate models, LockingMultiRewards, and Blast-specific contracts such as BlastBox, BlastMagicLP, BlastCauldronV4, BlastGovernor, BlastDapp, and onboarding components. Staking and reward flows use nominal transfer amounts rather than verified balance deltas, allowing fee-on-transfer or rebasing assets to create liabilities exceeding actual holdings. BlastDapp additionally performs an unconditional call to a hardcoded points endpoint during construction, creating deployment fragility on unsupported or incompatible networks. These issues were confirmed by openai-codex/gpt-5.6-luna.

Our analysis also examined deployment libraries, scripts, toolkits, and tests as operational trust boundaries. The architecture relies on owner-controlled configuration for reward tokens, Blast integrations, fee recipients, oracle components, and master-contract whitelists. These controls provide flexibility but increase the importance of validated configuration, fail-closed oracle behavior, explicit asset compatibility restrictions, and revocation mechanisms that invalidate already-established authority. Remediation should prioritize Cauldron oracle safeguards and master-contract emergency disablement, followed by robust token accounting and defensive validation in MagicLP and Blast deployment paths.

Stale oracle price is trusted during oracle failure, enabling undercollateralized borrowing

This oracle-failure logic error causes the protocol to treat an unsuccessful oracle update as a valid valuation. When the oracle does not provide an updated rate, the Cauldron reuses the previously cached exchange rate without imposing a freshness limit, validity window, or other protective constraint. Solvency checks and borrowing operations then continue using a potentially outdated collateral valuation.

After a material decline in the collateral price, an attacker can borrow additional funds or increase an existing position while the oracle reports that no update is available. The stale, higher exchange rate may allow the position to pass the solvency check even though it is undercollateralized at the current market price. The resulting debt may become unrecoverable before liquidation can occur, creating a direct risk of protocol losses.

Affected Components: the CauldronV4 contract, specifically the updateExchangeRate(), _isSolvent(), solvent(), and borrow/repay logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing CauldronV4.sol, I confirmed the oracle-failure vulnerability is exploitable. In updateExchangeRate(), the contract assigns the oracle result to (updated, rate), but when updated is false it explicitly replaces rate with the cached exchangeRate without recording freshness, imposing a validity window, or disabling risk-increasing actions. The borrow() function uses the solvent modifier, which executes the borrow first and then calls updateExchangeRate() before checking _isSolvent(msg.sender, _exchangeRate). Consequently, if the oracle fails after a collateral price decline, borrowing is evaluated against the stale, higher cached rate and can increase an already undercollateralized position. The same issue affects cook() when ACTION_BORROW is used, because its final solvency check also accepts the fallback rate. isSolvent() independently relies on the cached exchangeRate. Although liquidations intentionally support oracle failure, the implementation provides no equivalent guard preventing borrowing during that condition. Therefore, an oracle outage combined with a price decline creates a clear path to undercollateralized debt.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Track the timestamp or validity window of the cached rate and reject new borrowing or other risk-increasing operations when the rate is stale. Alternatively, use a conservative fallback valuation and preserve liquidation availability without allowing additional debt under an invalid price.

Cauldron accepts a zero exchange rate from a successful oracle update

This oracle-validation flaw allows the Cauldron to store and use an exchange rate of zero when the oracle reports a successful update. The update path assigns the returned rate directly to storage without enforcing a nonzero value or checking whether the rate falls within a safe and expected range.

If a misconfigured or compromised oracle, or an unexpected oracle failure mode, returns updated=true with rate=0, the Cauldron may accept the value as authoritative. The solvency calculation uses the exchange rate when determining the collateral required for a position; a zero rate can therefore reduce the calculated debt requirement to zero and allow borrowing against collateral that cannot support the resulting liability.

Affected Components: the CauldronV4 contract, specifically updateExchangeRate() and _isSolvent(), together with position accounting and borrow/repay logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing CauldronV4.updateExchangeRate, _isSolvent, borrow, and the cook borrow path, I confirmed the oracle-validation vulnerability. updateExchangeRate directly assigns exchangeRate = rate whenever oracle.get(oracleData) returns updated = true, without checking that rate is nonzero or within a sane range. _isSolvent then multiplies the debt requirement by _exchangeRate, so a zero rate makes the right-hand side zero; any account with nonzero collateral and debt is consequently considered solvent. The borrow function's solvent modifier calls updateExchangeRate after executing the borrow, and the final solvency check therefore accepts a zero oracle rate. The cook path behaves similarly when borrowing and performing its final solvency check. Although ACTION_UPDATE_EXCHANGE_RATE can impose a caller-selected rate bound, this is not a protocol-level safeguard and direct updateExchangeRate and borrow calls do not enforce it. Thus, a misconfigured or compromised oracle returning updated=true and rate=0 can enable borrowing against inadequately valued collateral.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Reject zero and out-of-range rates, validate the oracle response before updating storage, and retain the last known-good rate or enter a fail-closed mode when sanity checks fail.

Whitelisting revocation does not disable existing master-contract associations

This access-control flaw makes master-contract revocation ineffective for associations that were established before the revocation. The whitelist is checked when a master contract is registered, but removing that contract from the whitelist does not appear to clear existing user associations or invalidate related approvals. DegenBox authorization can therefore continue to rely on stale masterContractOf state.

If an approved master contract is compromised, the owner may attempt to disable it by removing it from the whitelist, while the compromised contract remains authorized for users who previously associated with it. The contract may continue initiating permitted transfers or withdrawals on behalf of those users, defeating the intended emergency revocation mechanism and potentially enabling unauthorized asset movement.

Affected Components: the MasterContractManager and DegenBox contracts, specifically whitelist and masterContractOf state, the allowed() authorization path, and withdraw()/transfer authorization logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing MasterContractManager.whitelistMasterContract, registerProtocol, setMasterContractApproval, and DegenBox.allowed, I confirmed that whitelist removal does not revoke existing master-contract authority. whitelistMasterContract only updates whitelistedMasterContracts and emits an event; it does not clear masterContractOf entries or masterContractApproved approvals. The whitelist is checked only in the unsigned-approval branch of setMasterContractApproval, while signed approvals can remain valid regardless of the whitelist state. Subsequently, DegenBox.allowed obtains masterContractOf[msg.sender] and checks only masterContractApproved[masterContract][from], without verifying whitelistedMasterContracts[masterContract] or any revocation epoch. Therefore, a registered master contract or clone whose address remains associated can continue invoking withdraw, transfer, transferMultiple, and deposit on behalf of users who previously approved it after the owner sets its whitelist status to false. No owner-controlled function in the reviewed code invalidates those associations or approvals, so whitelist removal is not an effective emergency disable mechanism for a compromised master contract.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Make DegenBox authorization require that the associated master contract remains currently whitelisted, or maintain a per-master-contract revocation epoch checked by DegenBox. Provide an owner-controlled mechanism to invalidate existing associations and approvals when a master contract is disabled.

Malformed MagicLP aggregator responses can permanently block oracle-dependent Cauldron operations

This denial-of-service vulnerability arises because the MagicLP price aggregator does not adequately validate underlying feed answers, token decimals, pool supply, or arithmetic bounds before calculating the LP token price. Signed or otherwise invalid feed values may be converted into unsigned integers, while zero total supply and unsafe scaling operations can cause arithmetic failures under Solidity 0.8.

An invalid or malformed response from an underlying feed, an uninitialized or depleted pool, or an input that exceeds the supported arithmetic range can cause the aggregator to revert. Because the aggregator is used during Cauldron exchange-rate updates, the failure can propagate to borrowing, liquidation, and solvency checks, potentially preventing users and keepers from performing oracle-dependent operations.

Affected Components: the MagicLpAggregator contract, specifically latestAnswer(), and the CauldronV4 contract, specifically updateExchangeRate() and the oracle-rate calculation

QA Review: Upon reviewing MagicLpAggregator.latestAnswer and CauldronV4.updateExchangeRate, I confirmed a denial-of-service path when the configured oracle or MagicLP pair enters an invalid state. latestAnswer explicitly casts baseOracle.latestAnswer() and quoteOracle.latestAnswer() from int256 to uint256 without checking positivity; a negative answer reverts under Solidity 0.8. The expressions 10 ** (WAD - decimals) can also revert when decimals exceed 18, and the unchecked-by-validation multiplications, addition, and final int256 conversion may overflow. Most directly, division by pair.totalSupply() reverts when totalSupply is zero. These failures propagate through oracle.get() into updateExchangeRate(), which has no try/catch or fallback. Consequently, borrow and solvency-checking paths, including borrow, removeCollateral, cook post-checks, and the liquidate path, can revert whenever they query the malformed aggregator. The source also contains an apparent _getReserves defect: it reads reserves into local variables but never returns them, causing default zero reserves, although this does not eliminate the division-by-zero and malformed-feed failure modes. No relevant guards exist in either contract.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require positive and fresh underlying answers, validate decimals and bounded magnitudes, reject zero totalSupply before division, use checked intermediate bounds, and define a non-reverting fallback or emergency oracle path for invalid pool/feed states.

Fee-on-transfer staking tokens can make internal principal accounting exceed actual holdings

This accounting error can occur when the LockingMultiRewards contract records the nominal staking amount without verifying the amount actually received. Fee-on-transfer tokens may transfer less than the requested amount, while rebasing tokens may later reduce the contract’s balance after staking. If the contract does not use balance-delta accounting or restrict unsupported token types, its internal principal records can exceed the tokens it actually holds.

An attacker or administrator can configure a non-standard staking token and cause users to stake amounts that are credited in full despite a transfer fee or subsequent balance reduction. Across multiple deposits and withdrawals, the contract may then be unable to satisfy recorded principal claims, causing withdrawal shortfalls, insolvency, or unfair losses among users.

Affected Components: the LockingMultiRewards contract, specifically staking-token transfer handling and stake/_stakeFor() accounting

QA Review: Upon reviewing the staking and withdrawal paths in `src/staking/LockingMultiRewards.sol`, I confirmed the accounting vulnerability. In `_stakeFor` (approximately lines 377-398), the contract calls `stakingToken.safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount)` and then unconditionally increments `stakingTokenBalance`, the user balance, and either `unlockedSupply` or `lockedSupply` by the nominal `amount`. It never measures the contract’s staking-token balance before and after the transfer. Consequently, a fee-on-transfer token can cause the contract to receive less than the amount credited internally, while a rebasing token can later reduce the actual balance without adjusting accounting. The `withdraw` function (approximately lines 166-183) similarly transfers and decrements the nominal amount, so withdrawals may revert once recorded liabilities exceed actual holdings, leaving user funds inaccessible; the issue can also affect subsequent users. No staking-token compatibility restriction or balance-delta validation exists in the constructor or staking path. `recover` does not mitigate this and may itself underflow when actual holdings are below `stakingTokenBalance`.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Either restrict stakingToken to known non-rebasing, non-fee-on-transfer tokens or calculate the received amount from pre- and post-transfer balances and use that actual amount consistently in total-supply, user-balance, and withdrawal accounting.

Fee-on-transfer reward tokens can create unpayable reward liabilities

This accounting error can arise when reward liabilities are calculated from the nominal amount specified during reward notification rather than the amount actually received by the contract. A fee-on-transfer reward token may deliver less than the notified amount, and a rebasing token may subsequently reduce the balance available for distribution. Without balance-delta validation or explicit token compatibility restrictions, the contract can record rewards that it does not possess.

An administrator can configure an unsupported reward token and notify a reward amount that exceeds the contract’s actual receipt. Users will accrue claims based on the nominal value, but later reward transfers may fail or leave the contract unable to pay all accrued liabilities. The issue can therefore cause claim failures, underpayment, or insolvency of the reward pool.

Affected Components: the LockingMultiRewards contract, specifically reward-token configuration and the addReward()/notifyRewardAmount() funding and accounting logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing the reward-token configuration and funding flow in LockingMultiRewards, I confirmed the accounting vulnerability. The owner may register arbitrary ERC20-compatible tokens through addReward, and notifyRewardAmount accepts any registered token without compatibility checks. In notifyRewardAmount, the contract records the requested amount, transfers it using rewardToken.safeTransferFrom, and then calculates rewardRate from that same nominal amount. SafeTransferLib validates transfer execution but does not measure the contract's balance before and after the transfer. Consequently, a fee-on-transfer token can deliver less than amount while the full nominal value is scheduled for distribution. Likewise, a rebasing token can reduce the contract's balance after notification. The existing-period remainder is also added using accounting values rather than verified token balances. As rewards accrue, users can receive liabilities exceeding the tokens held by the contract; eventual safeTransfer calls in _getRewards may revert or become economically insufficient. No balance-delta validation, token whitelist, or rejection of fee-on-transfer/rebasing assets is present. The issue depends on selecting such a token, but the code provides a clear exploitable deployment/configuration path.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Measure the reward-token balance delta when funding and calculate the reward schedule from the actual received amount; alternatively reject fee-on-transfer and rebasing reward tokens during configuration.

Hardcoded Blast Points endpoint can make deployments fail outside Blast

This availability issue results from the BlastDapp constructor making an unconditional external call to a hardcoded, chain-specific Blast Points endpoint. The deployment path does not appear to verify the chain ID, confirm that the target contains compatible contract code, or accept a validated endpoint configuration for other supported environments.

If the contract is deployed on a non-Blast network, an unsupported fork, or a network where the hardcoded address is absent or incompatible, the constructor call can revert and deployment will fail atomically. Although the issue does not directly compromise funds, it can prevent deployments and complicate testing or reuse of the contract outside the intended network.

Affected Components: the BlastDapp contract constructor and the BlastPoints library, specifically configure()

QA Review: Upon reviewing the BlastDapp constructor in src/blast/BlastDapp.sol and BlastPoints.configure in src/blast/libraries/BlastPoints.sol, I confirmed the deployment-fragility issue. The constructor unconditionally invokes BlastPoints.configure(), which performs an external call to the hardcoded BLAST_POINTS address 0x2536...d800 and passes the hardcoded operator address 0xD102...ff28. There is no chain ID validation, code-existence check, configurable endpoint, or error handling around this call. Consequently, deploying BlastDapp on a chain where that address contains incompatible code, implements a reverting function, or otherwise rejects configurePointsOperator will cause the constructor transaction to revert and the deployment to fail atomically. An address with no code may not itself cause a revert for a no-return call, so failure is not guaranteed solely by deploying on another chain; however, the implementation provides no protection against endpoint incompatibility and assumes the Blast deployment environment unconditionally. This is therefore a valid low-severity availability and portability issue, primarily affecting deployments and unsupported networks rather than an already-deployed contract.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Restrict deployment to the intended Blast chain, validate the chain ID and target code before calling, and make the points endpoint an immutable constructor parameter selected from a deployment-time allowlist. If points configuration is optional, separate it from construction and handle failure explicitly.

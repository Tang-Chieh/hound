Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Notional protocol and its leveraged-vault, proxy, integration, and accounting components.

The review covered the system architecture, including user and vault entrypoints, internal portfolio and balance accounting, valuation and settlement flows, upgrade boundaries, and integrations with Aave, Compound, Chainlink, Balancer, Aura, and decentralized-exchange routers.

Our team confirmed a high-severity configuration-integrity weakness in aTokenAggregator: independently supplied Aave addresses are not fully cross-validated, allowing an inconsistent lending pool and aToken pairing to produce an incorrect exchange rate. We also identified a medium-severity potential denial-of-service condition in settlement-data decoding, although exploitability against shared operations depends on surrounding call flows not included in the reviewed material. The overall security posture is dependent on strict deployment governance, trusted external integrations, and defensive validation at settlement boundaries.

System Overview

The Notional core protocol maintains account, portfolio, and balance state through components such as TransferAssets, BitmapAssetsHandler, AccountContextHandler, PortfolioState, BalanceHandler, and Notional account and balance storage. User-facing actions including LiquidateCurrencyAction interact with account context and balance machinery, while NotionalViews exposes stored state for valuation and monitoring. These flows make storage consistency, asset bitmap updates, and settlement ordering important security properties.

The leveraged-vaults subsystem is centered on VaultAction, VaultAccount, VaultState, VaultStateStorage, IVaultController, and IStrategyVault. VaultAction routes operations to strategy vault implementations, while VaultAccount uses AssetRate and NotionalCalculations for debt and collateral valuation. Concrete strategies such as Boosted3TokenAuraVault and CrossCurrencyfCashVault rely on PoolMixin, StrategyUtils, SettlementUtils, BalancerVaultStorage, and Balancer integrations to manage deposits, trades, rewards, repayment, and settlement.

External trust boundaries are significant throughout the architecture. CompoundHandler and CompoundToNotionalV2 interact with Compound interfaces; AaveHandler and aTokenAggregator depend on ILendingPool and IAToken; AssetRate uses ChainlinkAdapter and Chainlink aggregator interfaces; and strategy components use trading, Balancer, Aura, Uniswap V2, and Uniswap V3 interfaces. Our analysis revealed that these adapters must validate both returned values and the relationship among configured addresses, because downstream accounting may treat external responses as authoritative exchange rates or trade outcomes.

The Hound team identified a confirmed weakness in aTokenAggregator construction: the configured lending pool is not verified to recognize the supplied aToken as the reserve token for the underlying asset. A mismatched but operational configuration can therefore cause normalized income from one reserve or deployment to be used as the exchange rate for another token. The settlement layer also directly ABI-decodes caller-provided settlement and secondary trade data in SettlementUtils; malformed data can force reverts and may disrupt settlement, liquidation, redemption, or deleveraging paths where failures are not isolated.

Upgradeability and administrative control form an additional security boundary. Proxy, ERC1967Upgrade, UUPSUpgradeable, PauseRouter, EmptyProxy, nBeaconProxy, and nUpgradeableBeacon govern implementation dispatch and upgrade behavior. These mechanisms should be protected by tightly controlled authorization, validated implementation and beacon configuration, and operational pause procedures. The Hound team recommends enforcing immutable or registry-based integration configuration, cross-checking Aave reserve relationships during deployment, validating settlement payload structure before decoding, and ensuring individual malformed requests cannot block shared protocol operations.

Unvalidated Aave asset configuration can create an incorrect exchange-rate oracle

This oracle-integrity vulnerability results from insufficient validation of the relationship between the configured aToken, its underlying asset, and the associated Aave lending pool reserve. The aTokenAggregator constructor stores these external addresses independently and reads only limited metadata, such as the underlying asset's decimals and the aToken symbol, without verifying that the addresses represent a consistent Aave deployment. Both the stateful and view-based exchange-rate functions subsequently rely on this stored configuration.

A deployer, governance-controlled factory, or compromised configuration authority could provide mutually inconsistent Aave addresses, causing the aggregator to calculate or expose an incorrect exchange rate. Downstream deposit, withdrawal, collateral, or valuation logic that trusts this rate could misprice assets and potentially enable financial loss or manipulation. The risk is increased where the aggregator configuration is used as an oracle without additional validation or independent safeguards.

Affected Components: the aTokenAggregator contract, specifically the constructor(), getExchangeRateStateful(), and getExchangeRateView() functions, together with its AavePoolInterface and ATokenInterface integrations

QA Review: Upon reviewing the constructor and exchange-rate functions in contracts-v2/contracts/external/adapters/aTokenAggregator.sol, I confirmed a configuration-integrity vulnerability. The constructor derives `underlying` from `_aToken.UNDERLYING_ASSET_ADDRESS()`, but it never verifies that the supplied `_lendingPool` recognizes `address(_aToken)` as the reserve’s `aTokenAddress` for that underlying. Consequently, a valid aToken from one Aave deployment or reserve can be paired with a different lending pool, causing `_getNotionalExchangeRate()` to use `LendingPool.getReserveNormalizedIncome(underlying)` from an unrelated reserve while representing the exchange rate of the stored token. `getAnnualizedSupplyRate()` has the same unchecked pool dependency through `getReserveData(underlying)`. The constructor also lacks explicit nonzero-address checks, although calls to invalid contracts may revert rather than create a usable oracle. No validation or registry constraint appears in the provided code, so a deployer, governance-controlled factory, or compromised configuration process could install an aggregator with mutually inconsistent addresses. Exploitation therefore depends on configuration authority and downstream acceptance, but the unsafe oracle construction is clearly present.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): During construction, verify the aToken's underlying asset against the configured underlying, verify the lending pool's reserve configuration and aToken address for that underlying, reject zero or inconsistent addresses, and use an immutable trusted deployment registry where possible.

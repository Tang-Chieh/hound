Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Malt protocol, reviewing its principal contracts, interfaces, token flows, market mechanisms, reward systems, and administrative controls.

The review covered the high-level system architecture, including token transfer verification, bonding and mining, reward distribution, auctions and stabilization, decentralized-exchange liquidity and collateral services, market-data and time-series inputs, and external ERC-20, Uniswap, and governance integrations.

Our team confirmed several material security issues, including zero-minimum-output swaps, manipulable reserve-ratio sampling, persistent token allowances, balance-accounting errors in authorized trading and reward reinvestment flows, callback-based bonding disruption, and unsafe initialization or integration assumptions. Several proposed issues were rejected after review because existing access controls, bounded processing, or configuration invariants prevented the described exploit. Overall, the protocol has meaningful protective controls, but the confirmed findings require remediation before the system can be considered robust against market manipulation, privileged-component compromise, and configuration or integration failures.

System Overview

The Malt protocol is centered on the Malt token and uses TransferService as a verification boundary for token movements. Malt delegates transfer checks to configured ITransferVerification implementations, including PoolTransferVerification, which reads market information from MaltDataLab before allowing relevant transfers. This creates a security-sensitive boundary between token operations, transfer policy, administrator-managed verifier configuration, and price-dependent restrictions.

Bonding, MiningService, AbstractRewardMine, and ERC20VestedMine form the staking and reward-accounting subsystem. Bonding manages stake or LP-token positions and invokes MiningService, which synchronously forwards bond and unbond callbacks to registered reward mines. RewardDistributor, RewardThrottle, RewardOverflowPool, and ForfeitHandler coordinate reward allocation, throttling, overflow handling, and forfeiture. Our analysis revealed that a reverting registered reward mine can block bonding operations, while RewardReinvestor can attribute pre-existing reward-token balances to the caller because its reinvestment accounting is not isolated to the withdrawal delta.

Auction and stabilization components coordinate supply correction and liquidity operations. StabilizerNode interacts with Auction, IDexHandler, RewardThrottle, LiquidityExtension, ImpliedCollateralService, and SwingTrader, while Auction relies on market data and an optional auction-start controller. The UniswapHandler uses UniswapV2Library and current pool reserves to execute trades. We discovered that buyMalt and the corresponding sell path accept zero minimum output, exposing protocol-funded trades to adverse execution and sandwiching. The handler also transfers its entire post-swap Malt balance to the authorized caller, allowing pre-existing inventory to be swept. StabilizerNode additionally leaves residual reward-token allowances authorized to Auction after allocation.

MaltDataLab samples Uniswap reserves and writes observations to MovingAverage, which supplies time-series data to stabilization, liquidity, and collateral calculations. Permissionless reserve-ratio sampling can record manipulated spot reserves, and initialization with a zero moving-average sample length can make oracle updates and reads revert. The architecture also contains deployment-sensitive assumptions: UniswapV2Library relies on a hardcoded pair init-code hash, while reserve and pricing calculations trust external token decimal metadata without an explicit safe bound. These issues can cause incorrect pricing, unavailable reserve-dependent operations, or broader stabilization disruption when deployment parameters or external integrations are unsafe.

Uniswap buy path accepts zero minimum output

This slippage-protection logic error allows the Uniswap buy path to execute with zero minimum output. The buyMalt() function calls swapExactTokensForTokens with amountOutMin set to zero and relies solely on the pool's current reserves, without enforcing a caller- or protocol-supplied price limit or bounded slippage tolerance.

A searcher can observe a pending buyMalt transaction, temporarily trade against the Malt/reward pool to worsen the execution price, and allow the transaction to execute at the manipulated rate before reversing the position. This can cause the stabilizer to receive materially less Malt than intended and may amplify losses for the protocol. Equivalent protections should also be applied to the sell path.

Affected Components: the UniswapHandler contract, specifically buyMalt(), and the StabilizerNode contract, specifically stabilize()

QA Review: Upon reviewing UniswapHandler.buyMalt in src/contracts/DexHandlers/UniswapHandler.sol, I confirmed that the alleged slippage protection vulnerability exists. The function obtains the handler's entire rewardToken balance, approves the router, and calls swapExactTokensForTokens with amountOutMin set explicitly to zero. Consequently, any amount of Malt returned by the pool is accepted, subject only to the router's basic execution requirements. The deadline is now, but this does not constrain the execution price or prevent same-block transaction ordering and sandwich manipulation. StabilizerNode._startAuction invokes swingTrader.buyMalt before triggering an auction, while the trade size is calculated from pool reserves and a target price; neither that calculation nor StabilizerNode.stabilize supplies an execution-time minimum output to the handler. A searcher can therefore observe a pending authorized buy, trade against the Malt/reward pool to worsen the effective rate, allow the buy to execute, and potentially unwind the position afterward. The sellMalt function also uses amountOutMin zero, indicating the same missing control on the reverse path.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require a nonzero minimum output derived from a trusted price limit and bounded slippage, or use a deadline and explicit min-output parameter supplied by the protected caller. Apply equivalent protections to the sell path.

Permissionless reserve-ratio sampling enables flash manipulation of the moving average

This oracle-manipulation vulnerability allows any account to update a security-sensitive moving average using manipulable spot reserves. MaltDataLab.trackReserveRatio() is permissionless and obtains the sampled value from LiquidityExtension.reserveRatio(), which is derived from the current Uniswap reserves without access control, deviation limits, or validation against a trusted observation source.

An attacker can use a flash swap or a large temporary trade to distort the pool reserves, call trackReserveRatio() while the manipulation is active, and then reverse the trade. The manipulated observation can influence reserve-ratio calculations and downstream stabilization decisions. Sampling should be restricted or protected with minimum intervals, deviation checks, and a manipulation-resistant oracle such as a suitable TWAP.

Affected Components: the MaltDataLab contract, specifically trackReserveRatio(), the LiquidityExtension contract, specifically reserveRatio(), and the StabilizerNode contract

QA Review: Upon reviewing MaltDataLab.trackReserveRatio and LiquidityExtension.reserveRatio, I confirmed that the reported oracle-manipulation condition exists. trackReserveRatio is public and has no caller restriction, timing requirement, deviation bound, or trusted-source comparison. It obtains the ratio directly from liquidityExtension.reserveRatio(), which computes the value from the current Uniswap pair reserves returned by UniswapV2Library.getReserves. Those reserves can be temporarily distorted through a large swap or flash-swap transaction, after which the attacker can invoke trackReserveRatio while the manipulated state is observable, then unwind the trade in the same transaction. The sampled value is passed directly to reserveRatioMA.update without validation. StabilizerNode also invokes trackReserveRatio during stabilization paths, confirming that this data is part of protocol operations, although the supplied code does not include the moving-average implementation or every downstream consumer, so the ultimate economic impact and persistence constraints cannot be fully quantified. Existing role checks protect other sampling functions but do not protect this function, and no manipulation-resistant oracle mechanism is visible in the reviewed code.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Restrict sampling to authorized keepers or a controller, enforce minimum time between observations, reject implausible deviations, and use a manipulation-resistant oracle or TWAP whose observation cannot be changed atomically with the sampled trade.

RewardReinvestor Can Attribute Pre-existing Reward Balance to an Arbitrary Account

This accounting error allows RewardReinvestor to attribute pre-existing reward tokens to an arbitrary account. The reinvestment flow withdraws rewards through MiningService, converts tokens through a DEX handler, and stakes the resulting Malt for a caller-supplied account, but does not reliably isolate the amount withdrawn for that account from any reward-token balance already held by RewardReinvestor.

An attacker can leave or transfer reward tokens to the reinvestor and then call reinvest() with an attacker-controlled beneficiary. If the implementation swaps the contract's aggregate balance, the attacker can cause residual funds to be converted and staked on their behalf. The function should calculate the withdrawal delta using before-and-after balances, swap only that delta, and bind the staking beneficiary to the account whose rewards were actually withdrawn.

Affected Components: the RewardReinvestor contract, specifically reinvest(), the IMiningService interface, the IDexHandler interface, and the IBonding interface

QA Review: Upon reviewing the RewardReinvestor functions provideReinvest, splitReinvest, _retrieveReward, and _bondAccount, I confirmed the accounting error is exploitable. Both public reinvestment paths withdraw rewards for msg.sender, but then calculate or transfer the contract’s entire rewardToken balance rather than the amount received during that call. In provideReinvest, rewardBalance is set directly to rewardToken.balanceOf(address(this)); in splitReinvest, half of the entire balance is sent to dexHandler and _bondAccount subsequently transfers the remaining entire balance. _bondAccount supplies all contract-held assets to dexHandler and bonds the resulting liquidity using bonding.bondToAccount(account), where account is msg.sender. Therefore, an attacker can ensure reward tokens are already held by RewardReinvestor, call either function with a positive rewardLiquidity corresponding to their own rewards, and receive credit for liquidity generated from both their withdrawal and the pre-existing balance. The only guard rejects zero withdrawals and does not isolate the withdrawal delta. Any residual assets are swept to treasury only after they have been included in the attacker’s bond.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Record the reward-token balance before withdrawal, calculate the post-withdrawal delta, and swap only that delta. Ensure the staking beneficiary is bound to the account whose rewards were withdrawn, and separately handle or explicitly sweep residual tokens under a restricted administrative process.

Hardcoded Pair Init Code Hash Can Route Reserve Reads to a Nonexistent or Wrong Pair

This integration error causes Uniswap pair addresses to be derived from a hardcoded pair init-code hash rather than being authoritatively obtained from the configured factory. The UniswapV2Library.pairFor() and getReserves() functions therefore assume that every configured factory uses the exact pair bytecode represented by the fixed hash, without validating the result against factory.getPair().

If an administrator configures a compatible-looking factory with a different init-code hash, or the protocol is deployed against a different Uniswap-compatible implementation, reserve and pricing calls may target a nonexistent or incorrect pair. This can cause calls to revert or cause the protocol to consume inaccurate reserve data. Initialization should validate the factory and derived pair addresses, or use the factory's pair lookup as the authoritative source.

Affected Components: the UniswapV2Library, specifically pairFor() and getReserves(), the UniswapHandler contract, specifically reserves(), the LiquidityExtension contract, specifically collateralDeficit(), and the Uniswap reserve data source

QA Review: Upon reviewing UniswapV2Library.pairFor and getReserves, I confirmed the integration error exists. pairFor deterministically computes the pair address using a fixed init-code hash (the hex value embedded in UniswapV2Library.sol) and performs no call to the configured factory's getPair function. This derived address is then used by getReserves, which directly invokes IUniswapV2Pair(pair).getReserves(). UniswapHandler.initialize and LiquidityExtension.initialize accept arbitrary factory addresses but do not validate the factory, its pair-code hash, or the existence and token composition of the derived pair. Consequently, deploying either contract with a Uniswap-compatible fork or factory using different pair bytecode causes reserve reads to target an unrelated address or a nonexistent contract, potentially reverting all dependent pricing, reserve-ratio, and collateral-deficit operations. If an address containing compatible pair-like code happens to exist, incorrect reserves could instead be consumed. The issue is conditional on privileged deployment misconfiguration rather than an unprivileged attacker, but the source clearly lacks the required invariant validation.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use the factory's pair lookup as the authoritative address, or validate at initialization that the configured factory's pair matches the derived address and reject incompatible factories. Verify pair existence before consuming reserves.

External Token Decimal Metadata Can Cause Arithmetic Reverts in Reserve Pricing and Deficit Calculations

This denial-of-service arithmetic error occurs because reserve pricing and deficit calculations trust external ERC20 decimals() values without constraining the scaling range. The affected logic performs exponentiation and multiplication using the reported decimal count, which can overflow when a configured token reports an excessively large or otherwise unsupported value.

If a token with an excessive decimals value is configured as the reward or collateral token, calls to market-price, reserve-ratio, or collateral-deficit functions may revert. Such failures can block reserve-dependent stabilization and liquidity operations. Token decimal values should be validated during configuration, and all scaling operations should use bounded arithmetic with explicit overflow and supported-range checks.

Affected Components: the UniswapHandler contract, specifically maltMarketPrice(), the LiquidityExtension contract, specifically collateralDeficit(), and the Uniswap reserve data source

QA Review: Upon reviewing the decimal-scaling logic in UniswapHandler.maltMarketPrice, MaltDataLab._normalizedPrice, and LiquidityExtension.collateralDeficit and reserveRatio, I confirmed that excessive token metadata can cause reserve-dependent calls to revert. These functions obtain decimals() from externally configured ERC20 contracts and use the result directly in 10**decimals and SafeMath multiplications, without validating a supported range. Although the OpenZeppelin ERC20 interface returns uint8, a token can still report a value such as 255, and the resulting exponentiation or subsequent multiplication by reserves will generally overflow or produce invalid scaling; malformed return data can also make the decimals() call revert. The same issue affects hasMinimumReserves through reserveRatio. Token addresses are supplied during privileged initialization, so this is not an arbitrary permissionless attack against an already correctly configured deployment, but governance or deployment of a malicious/nonstandard token can permanently make public views and updater workflows unusable. No initialization or configuration guard rejects unsafe decimals values, and SafeMath only converts the overflow into a revert rather than preventing the denial of service.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Validate token decimals against a safe maximum during initialization/configuration, use bounded scaling with explicit overflow checks, and reject unsupported decimal combinations before enabling reserve-dependent operations.

buyMalt Sweeps Pre-existing Malt Balance to the Buyer

This accounting error causes buyMalt() to transfer the handler's entire Malt balance rather than only the tokens received from the current swap. After the swap, the function uses malt.balanceOf(address(this)) as the purchased amount and transfers that full balance to msg.sender, without recording the handler's balance before the transaction.

If the UniswapHandler holds Malt from an earlier operation or another source, an account with BUYER_ROLE can cause those pre-existing tokens to be transferred along with the current purchase. Exploitation requires an authorized or compromised caller, but the flaw allows retained inventory to be drained and undermines the intended trade accounting. The function should transfer only the post-swap balance increase and separately manage any pre-existing inventory.

Affected Components: the UniswapHandler contract, specifically buyMalt(), the StabilizerNode contract, specifically its buyMalt() caller, and the Malt token

QA Review: Upon reviewing UniswapHandler.buyMalt in src/contracts/DexHandlers/UniswapHandler.sol, I confirmed the accounting error exists. The function records the handler's reward-token balance, swaps that balance through the router with the Malt output sent to address(this), and then sets purchased = malt.balanceOf(address(this)) before transferring the entire value to msg.sender. It never records the Malt balance before the swap or calculates the post-swap increase. Consequently, any Malt already held by the handler—whether accidentally transferred, retained from another operation, or introduced through an integration flow—is swept to the authorized caller along with the current trade output. onlyRole(BUYER_ROLE) is a meaningful access control and prevents arbitrary public callers, but it does not distinguish legitimate purchased output from pre-existing inventory; therefore, a compromised, misconfigured, or otherwise authorized buyer can extract that inventory by calling buyMalt. The issue is not demonstrated in StabilizerNode itself, but the handler implementation clearly contains the vulnerable balance accounting and should use a before/after delta or reject nonzero pre-swap Malt balances.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Record the Malt balance before the swap, compute the post-swap increase, and transfer only that delta. Separately account for or reject pre-existing Malt inventory, and ensure BUYER_ROLE is assigned only to the intended stabilization contract.

Residual RewardToken Allowance Remains Authorized to Auction

This access-control and allowance-management weakness leaves the auction contract with a persistent RewardToken allowance after reward distribution. StabilizerNode._distributeRewards() approves the auction to spend the rewarded amount before calling auction.allocateArbRewards(), but does not visibly clear unused approval after the external call.

If the auction contract is compromised, incorrectly configured, or able to invoke transferFrom() outside the intended allocation flow, it may use any residual allowance to transfer RewardToken held by StabilizerNode, including tokens received later. The allowance should be limited to the exact amount consumed and reset to zero after allocation, with the allocation result verified before the operation completes.

Affected Components: the StabilizerNode contract, specifically _distributeRewards(), the Auction contract, specifically allocateArbRewards(), and the RewardToken token

QA Review: Upon reviewing StabilizerNode._distributeRewards and Auction.allocateArbRewards, I confirmed the residual-allowance vulnerability exists. StabilizerNode calls rewardToken.approve(address(auction), rewarded) before invoking auction.allocateArbRewards(rewarded), but never resets the allowance afterward or verifies the amount actually consumed. Auction.allocateArbRewards may return without transferring any tokens when the replenishing auction is invalid, has no commitments, or already has sufficient claimable tokens, leaving the full approval outstanding. Even when it transfers only a fraction through collateralToken.safeTransferFrom(stabilizerNode, address(this), maxArbAllocation), the unused allowance remains authorized. The allowance is not limited to the current transaction and can apply to reward tokens subsequently held by StabilizerNode. Auction's STABILIZER_NODE_ROLE check restricts the intended implementation, but it does not protect against a compromised, replaceable, or otherwise malicious auction implementation using transferFrom. The existing code therefore creates a persistent authorization from StabilizerNode to the auction and should clear or tightly reconcile the allowance after allocation.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use an exact-transfer pattern that sets allowance to zero after allocation, or approve only the amount actually consumed. Prefer safeIncreaseAllowance/safeDecreaseAllowance where appropriate and verify the auction spent exactly the intended amount.

Zero Sample Length Can Permanently Revert Moving-Average Updates

This denial-of-service configuration error allows a moving-average sample length of zero to be stored without validation. The moving-average implementation relies on a circular buffer and average calculations that commonly use the sample length for modulo and division operations; with a zero-length buffer, subsequent updates or reads can revert.

A compromised or misconfigured administrator can therefore disable MaltDataLab sampling and average retrieval by setting the sample length to zero. Configuration setters should require a positive value and enforce a reasonable maximum, while update and read functions should defensively reject invalid state. Sensitive parameter changes should also be subject to appropriate governance controls.

Affected Components: the MovingAverage contract, specifically its sample-length configuration, circular-buffer update, and average calculation logic; the MaltDataLab contract, specifically moving-average sample writes; and the IMovingAverage interface

QA Review: Upon reviewing the MovingAverage initializer, configuration methods, and arithmetic paths, I confirmed that zero sample length can put the contract into a denial-of-service state when supplied during initialization. initialize() validates _sampleMemory but does not validate _sampleLength before storing it, whereas setSampleLength() later correctly requires _sampleLength > 0. With sampleLength equal to zero, getValueWithLookback() immediately executes modulo sampleLength and reverts. Subsequent update() calls also encounter invalid zero-length arithmetic, including (sampleLength - 1) under SafeMath and divisions by sampleLength in _createNewSample(); updateCumulative() similarly reaches division by sampleLength or other zero-length logic. MaltDataLab forwards tracking operations directly to these update functions, so affected oracle updates can revert, and its smoothed-read methods can revert through getValueWithLookback(). The issue is limited to an administrator or deployment process supplying zero during initialize(), since the normal setSampleLength() setter rejects zero, and it is recoverable by setting a positive value. Nevertheless, the missing initialization guard provides a clear misconfiguration or compromised-administrator DoS path.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require sampleLength > 0 and enforce a sane maximum in every configuration setter; reject invalid values before storing them. Add defensive checks in update and average functions so invalid configuration cannot cause protocol-wide reverts, and use a timelocked governance change for parameter updates.

Registered Reward Mine Revert Can Denial-of-Service Bonding Operations

This denial-of-service vulnerability results from synchronous callbacks to all registered reward mines without failure isolation or a circuit breaker. MiningService invokes registered IRewardMine implementations during operations initiated by Bonding, so a single mine that reverts can propagate the failure through the entire callback sequence.

A faulty or compromised reward mine, or a state transition that causes a registered mine to reject the callback, can prevent Bonding operations from completing. Because the mine set is administrator-managed, registration and removal controls are especially important. Callback execution should provide per-mine failure handling where safe, bounded execution, and an emergency mechanism to disable or remove a failing mine.

Affected Components: the MiningService contract, specifically reward-mine callback dispatch, the Bonding contract, specifically callbacks into MiningService, and the IRewardMine interface

QA Review: Upon reviewing MiningService.onBond and onUnbond and Bonding.bondToAccount, unbond, and unbondAndBreak, I confirmed the denial-of-service condition exists. MiningService iterates over every address in mines and synchronously invokes IRewardMine(mine).onBond or onUnbond without checking mineActive, using try/catch, imposing gas bounds, or isolating failures. Any revert from one registered mine therefore reverts the entire MiningService call. Bonding invokes these callbacks before updating user balances or transferring tokens, so a reverting mine prevents all bonding and unbonding operations that depend on the affected MiningService. addRewardMine accepts an arbitrary address and performs no interface or behavioral validation, making faulty or malicious implementations registrable by an administrator. Although removeRewardMine provides an administrative recovery mechanism, it does not prevent the disruption and may be unavailable during an administrative compromise or operational failure. The hypothesis is therefore technically valid, with exploitability depending on a mine being registered and made to revert; this is not a permissionless attack against the current code unless administrative control or a faulty registered mine is involved.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Use bounded callback dispatch with per-mine failure isolation where safe, provide an emergency pause/removal mechanism, validate mine contracts before registration, and ensure Bonding operations cannot be permanently blocked by one reward mine.

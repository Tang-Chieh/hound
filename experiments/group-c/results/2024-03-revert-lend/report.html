Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Revert Lend protocol, focusing on its lending vault, collateralized borrowing, liquidation, automated position management, token swaps, and supporting oracle and interest-rate components.

The review covered the system architecture and interaction flows among lenders, borrowers, V3Vault, V3Oracle, InterestRateModel, FlashloanLiquidator, Swapper, transformers, automators, Uniswap V3 integrations, and external token and pricing dependencies.

Our team identified four confirmed security concerns: unauthenticated flash-loan callback execution, insufficient transaction-specific slippage protection in AutoCompound pool swaps, transfer-stale AutoExit configurations, and ERC-4626 withdrawal-limit inconsistencies that can deny lender exits during illiquidity. Several additional risks, including balance accounting, router trust, reentrancy, and operational denial of service, require further validation or defense-in-depth improvements. The protocol has meaningful authorization, collateral-health, oracle-consistency, and position-approval controls, but the confirmed issues should be addressed before relying on the affected workflows in production.

System Overview

The Hound team analyzed Revert Lend's V3Vault as the central system boundary. The vault implements IVault and manages ERC-4626 lending shares, borrower debt shares, loan ownership, NFT collateral, borrowing, repayment, liquidation, and position transformations. Borrowing and repayment update debt accounting, while collateral-health checks use V3Oracle valuation and the configured InterestRateModel to maintain lending and debt exchange rates.

V3Oracle provides position breakdowns and collateral valuations using configured token pricing sources, including Chainlink feeds and Uniswap V3 TWAP mechanisms. The oracle also compares the collateral pool's current price against the independently derived token ratio, providing a consistency check against significant price divergence. InterestRateModel supplies per-second rates used by V3Vault to accrue lending and borrowing economics based on liquidity and outstanding debt.

Liquidation is available directly through V3Vault or through FlashloanLiquidator. The flash-liquidation path obtains liquidity from a Uniswap V3 pool, invokes the vault liquidation flow, swaps collateral through Swapper, and repays the flash loan. Our analysis revealed that the external uniswapV3FlashCallback boundary lacks demonstrated authentication to the configured pool and active liquidation operation, allowing callback logic to be reached outside a legitimate flash-loan context.

Transformers and automators operate on Uniswap V3 position NFTs held by the vault or externally owned. LeverageTransformer borrows from V3Vault, swaps assets through router integrations, and adjusts liquidity, while V3Utils handles position minting, transfers, and balance deltas. AutoCompound performs reward collection and pool swaps before reinvesting, but its pool-swap path uses a TWAP check without a transaction-specific minimum output. AutoExit stores execution configuration by token ID, creating a transfer-lifecycle issue when positions change ownership.

The Hound team also reviewed ERC-4626 lender-exit behavior. V3Vault uses an internally maintained lending exchange rate rather than raw donated balances for share conversion and incorporates debt into internal liquidity accounting. However, maxWithdraw, maxRedeem, and preview functions do not account for immediately transferable cash, while the actual withdrawal path checks available liquidity and can revert. This inconsistency may trap lender funds during periods when borrowers have reduced vault cash, even though the lending shares remain economically valued.

Flash callback may accept forged callbacks without pool and operation authentication

This access-control vulnerability arises because the external flash-loan callback is not demonstrably authenticated against both the trusted flash-loan pool and the liquidation operation currently in progress. The uniswapV3FlashCallback function decodes caller-supplied data and processes callback amounts, but without enforcing the expected pool address, callback parameters, and operation-specific state, an arbitrary contract may be able to invoke the callback directly or through a malicious pool-like contract.

An attacker could submit forged callback data and amounts to trigger liquidation or related token-handling logic outside the intended flash-loan flow. Depending on the implementation, this could result in unauthorized asset transfers, incorrect accounting, execution against an unintended liquidation target, or other loss of funds. The callback should require the caller to be the configured pool, validate pool provenance where applicable, and verify all expected tokens, amounts, and targets against a pending liquidation record that is cleared after execution.

Affected Components: the FlashloanLiquidator contract, specifically the uniswapV3FlashCallback() callback and liquidate() function

QA Review: Upon reviewing FlashloanLiquidator.liquidate and uniswapV3FlashCallback in src/utils/FlashloanLiquidator.sol, I confirmed an access-control vulnerability. The callback is externally callable and contains no check that msg.sender is the flashLoanPool selected by liquidate, no factory/provenance validation, and no pending-operation state binding the callback to an initiated liquidation. Although liquidate encodes callback data and invokes params.flashLoanPool.flash, uniswapV3FlashCallback can also be called directly or through an attacker-controlled pool-like contract with attacker-selected FlashCallbackData, fee values, vault, token, swap, and recipient parameters. The callback then approves the arbitrary vault, invokes vault.liquidate, performs swaps, and transfers the repayment amount plus supplied fees to msg.sender; it also transfers leftover tokens and rewards to data.liquidator. The inline comment explicitly acknowledges that no origin check is performed, but the claim that the contract holds no funds is not a sufficient security boundary because balances or liquidation proceeds can exist during execution. Consequently, an untrusted caller can drive callback logic outside a legitimate flash-loan context and potentially redirect assets or execute unauthorized liquidations.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require msg.sender to equal the configured flash-loan pool, validate factory/pool provenance where applicable, and bind the callback to an operation-specific pending state containing the expected pool, tokens, amounts, and liquidation target; clear that state after completion.

AutoExit configuration may remain bound to a token ID after NFT ownership changes

This access-control and lifecycle-management vulnerability may occur when AutoExit configuration is associated only with an NFT token ID and is not revalidated after ownership changes. An owner can configure an exit while holding the NFT, but operator execution may later rely on that stale configuration without confirming the current NFT owner or invalidating the configuration when the position is transferred. This allows configuration authority and execution authority to become detached from the current position owner.

An owner could configure an AutoExit position and then transfer the NFT, leaving the operator able to execute the former owner’s parameters against the transferred position. Depending on the payout and exit logic, proceeds could be directed to the former owner, or the position could be exited using obsolete thresholds, recipients, or other parameters. Execution should resolve and verify current NFT ownership, bind configuration to the current owner, invalidate configuration on transfer, and require the new owner to reauthorize payout recipients and exit parameters.

Affected Components: the AutoExit and Automator contracts, specifically the NFT owner configuration and operator-gated execution paths

QA Review: Upon reviewing AutoExit.execute() and configToken(), I confirmed that configuration is stored solely in positionConfigs[tokenId] and is not bound to the configuring owner or invalidated when ownership changes. configToken() checks nonfungiblePositionManager.ownerOf(tokenId) only at configuration time, while execute() loads the configuration by token ID without revalidating that the configuring owner remains the current owner. Although execution ultimately resolves ownerOf(tokenId) and sends proceeds to the current owner, preventing the specific claim that funds are paid to the former owner, stale trigger, swap, slippage, and reward parameters can still be applied to a position after transfer. Execution also requires the AutoExit contract to remain approved by the current owner through the position manager, so exploitation is conditional on current-owner approval or an equivalent authorization path; the former owner’s token-specific approval is normally cleared on transfer. Nevertheless, the missing transfer-aware invalidation or owner binding creates a real lifecycle authorization flaw, allowing an operator to execute obsolete owner-selected instructions against the transferred position.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): At execution time, resolve and verify the current NFT owner and bind configuration to the current owner rather than only the token ID. Clear or invalidate configurations on NFT transfer, and require the current owner to reauthorize any payout recipient or exit parameters.

medium

TWAP-bound pool swaps may still permit economically harmful execution within the accepted deviation

This market-manipulation vulnerability results from relying on an oracle or TWAP price check without enforcing transaction-specific slippage and execution limits. Although the TWAP check can reject extreme price deviations, it may still permit a swap to execute at a materially unfavorable price if the manipulated price remains within the configured tolerance. The execution path also does not inherently provide minimum-output, maximum-input, deadline, or maximum-price-impact protections.

An attacker could monitor a pending AutoCompound execution, temporarily manipulate the relevant Uniswap V3 pool, and allow the swap to proceed at a price that satisfies the TWAP bound while still causing an economic loss. The attacker could then reverse the manipulation after execution, for example through a sandwich strategy. The swap should enforce caller- or owner-defined minimum output and maximum input values, use a short deadline, limit price impact, and validate the final execution price against both the TWAP and transaction-specific slippage constraints.

Affected Components: the AutoCompound contract, specifically executeWithVault(), and its pool-swap execution path, including the associated swapper logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing AutoCompound.execute and Swapper._poolSwap, I confirmed that the alleged market-manipulation exposure exists. When params.amountIn is nonzero, execute reads the pool's current slot0 tick and, if configured, only verifies it through _hasMaxTWAPTickDifference against the TWAP. If the check passes, it calls _poolSwap with amountOutMin explicitly set to zero. Although _poolSwap calculates amountOutDelta, its slippage guard is therefore ineffective because no minimum output is supplied. The Uniswap V3 swap also uses a near-unbounded sqrt price limit (TickMath.MIN_SQRT_RATIO + 1 or MAX_SQRT_RATIO - 1), rather than a transaction-specific execution-price constraint. Consequently, an attacker able to move the pool price before the transaction can keep the spot tick within the permitted TWAP deviation while worsening the exact-input swap, then reverse the trade afterward. The TWAP check limits larger deviations but does not guarantee fair execution, protect against sandwiching, enforce maximum price impact, or bind the result to an expected output. The router path has an amountOutMin check, but it is not used by AutoCompound's pool-swap path.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require caller-supplied or owner-configured minimum output and maximum input values, enforce a short deadline and maximum price impact, and validate the final execution price against both TWAP and transaction-specific slippage limits.

medium

ERC-4626 redemption can overstate liquidity when assets are lent out

This ERC-4626 accounting vulnerability occurs when the vault includes outstanding lending receivables or debt in total asset valuation while treating the same assets as immediately available for withdrawals and redemptions. Borrowing can therefore reduce the vault’s transferable cash without proportionally reducing the reported share value. If liquidity checks and withdrawal limits do not distinguish economic assets from currently available cash, standard ERC-4626 exits may be approved based on an inflated valuation but fail during execution.

A user or connected borrower could reduce available cash while debt remains included in the vault’s valuation, causing share holders to encounter systematic withdrawal or redemption reverts until repayment or liquidation restores liquidity. This can prevent otherwise valid exits and create an unfair liquidity burden for remaining lenders, particularly where lenders absorb liquidation deficits. The vault should calculate maxWithdraw() and maxRedeem() using transferable cash after reserves, or provide an explicit withdrawal queue and claim mechanism, with preview functions and execution paths using consistent liquidity-aware accounting.

Affected Components: the V3Vault contract, specifically its ERC-4626 lending-share accounting, borrow(), repay(), liquidate(), withdrawal, and redemption liquidity logic

QA Review: Upon reviewing V3Vault's ERC-4626 functions and liquidity accounting, I confirmed a real liquidity-denial issue. Borrowing in borrow() transfers assets out of the vault, while _getAvailableBalance() correctly computes transferable liquidity from the token balance after reserves. However, maxWithdraw() returns the owner's entire share value and maxRedeem() returns the owner's full share balance without considering available liquidity. Likewise, previewWithdraw() and previewRedeem() report amounts based solely on the lending exchange rate. The actual _withdraw() path subsequently checks available < assets and reverts with InsufficientLiquidity(), creating a direct inconsistency between ERC-4626 previews/max limits and executable withdrawals. A borrower can legitimately reduce cash liquidity while debt and lender claims remain outstanding, causing holders and integrators relying on maxWithdraw, maxRedeem, or previews to repeatedly submit reverting exit transactions until repayment or liquidation restores liquidity. This does not directly steal funds, and borrow authorization/collateral checks remain effective, but the code clearly permits systematic exit denial and violates the expected liquidity-aware semantics of the ERC-4626 limit functions.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Base maxWithdraw and maxRedeem on transferable cash after reserves, or implement an explicit withdrawal queue/claim mechanism; ensure preview functions and actual withdrawal paths use consistent liquidity-aware accounting.

Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the Lybra Protocol, reviewing its core collateralized stablecoin, governance, rewards, proxy, and cross-chain components.

The assessment covered the system architecture, including interactions among protocol users, governance and timelock administration, proxy-managed configuration, collateral vaults, EUSD and PeUSD token flows, liquidation mechanisms, rewards modules, price oracles, external collateral tokens, and LayerZero-based cross-chain services.

Our team identified several confirmed security issues affecting access control, reward accounting, oracle validation, staking incentives, and collateral accounting. The most significant risks include ineffective role enforcement in configurator protections, permanently unaccounted reward transfers when no users are staked, retroactive reward boosts, invalid LBR oracle acceptance, and stETH rebase-related insolvency exposure. These issues require remediation before the protocol can be considered robust against privileged-role compromise, malformed oracle data, adverse collateral behavior, and reward-accounting manipulation.

System Overview

The Lybra Protocol is organized around LybraProxy and LybraProxyAdmin, with the proxy routing calls to LybraConfigurator and related implementation contracts. LybraConfigurator maintains protocol-wide addresses, vault activation, mint limits, collateral parameters, token configuration, and reward distribution settings. GovernanceTimelock and LybraGovernance are intended to control privileged operations, while vaults and reward modules consume configuration values during normal execution.

Collateralized borrowing is implemented through LybraEUSDVaultBase and LybraPeUSDVaultBase. Concrete vaults, including LybraStETHDepositVault, LybraWstETHVault, LybraRETHVault, and LybraWBETHVault, accept collateral tokens, consult configured ETH or collateral price feeds, mint EUSD or PeUSD, and process withdrawals and liquidations. Liquidators repay stablecoin debt and receive collateral subject to collateral-ratio and liquidation limits. Our analysis also examined the effect of rebasing collateral: stETH positions are recorded using nominal token amounts rather than a rebasing-aware share representation, so an adverse rebase can reduce actual backing while recorded collateral and debt calculations remain unchanged.

EUSD and PeUSD form the principal stablecoin flows. Vaults mint and burn these tokens against collateralized positions, while PeUSD additionally implements OFTCoreV2 functionality for cross-chain transfers. OFTCoreV2 uses LayerZero application and endpoint interfaces to serialize transfers, send messages through the LayerZero service, and deliver value to remote-chain OFT contracts. The reviewed cross-chain amount conversion includes a checked uint64 boundary, so oversized transfers revert atomically rather than silently truncating source or destination value.

Rewards are distributed through EUSDMiningIncentives, ProtocolRewardsPool, StakingRewardsV2Pool, and esLBRBoost. EUSDMiningIncentives uses an LBR price oracle when users purchase other earnings, but the returned price is accepted without positivity, freshness, or round-completeness validation. ProtocolRewardsPool receives protocol distributions from LybraConfigurator, and our analysis revealed that transfers made while no esLBR is staked can remain stranded outside reward-per-token accounting. StakingRewardsV2Pool also applies the current boost across the full uncheckpointed reward interval, allowing a newly established esLBR lock to increase rewards accrued before the lock.

Governance and administrative security are critical boundaries in this architecture. We discovered that the configurator's role-checking integration does not enforce the boolean result returned by GovernanceTimelock, allowing protected configuration functions to be called without the intended authorization. Additional operational risks include zero reward durations causing later scheduling failures and truncation of unscheduled reward remainders. Together, these findings demonstrate that governance enforcement, oracle validation, reward accounting, and rebasing-aware collateral valuation should be strengthened before deployment in a high-value environment.

Deployer retains direct DAO authority over critical configurator controls

This access control vulnerability allows the deployment account to retain direct authority over critical DAO-only configurator functions. During construction, the governance timelock grants the DAO role to msg.sender, and the configurator's role checks only verify possession of that role; they do not require the caller to be the timelock contract or otherwise enforce governance execution through a delay.

As a result, the deployer, or an attacker who compromises the deployment account, can invoke privileged configuration functions directly rather than submitting an operation through the timelock. This bypasses the intended governance review and delay, allowing immediate modification of critical protocol parameters and increasing the risk of unauthorized or malicious configuration changes.

Affected Components: the GovernanceTimelock contract, specifically its constructor; the LybraConfigurator contract, specifically DAO-authorized configuration functions and their role checks; and the governance DAO authorization flow

QA Review: Upon reviewing the GovernanceTimelock constructor and Configurator access-control modifiers, I confirmed that the hypothesized privilege bypass exists, and the implementation contains an even broader flaw. GovernanceTimelock.sol grants DAO to both address(this) and msg.sender via _grantRole(DAO, ...), so the deployer receives DAO authority without any timelock delay. Configurator's onlyRole(DAO) modifier calls checkOnlyRole(DAO, msg.sender), which merely returns hasRole(role, _sender) and does not revert when false; the returned boolean is ignored. Consequently, initToken, setMintVault, setMintVaultMaxSupply, and setBadCollateralRatio are callable by any address, including the deployer directly, without governance execution or delay. The same issue affects the checkRole modifier and all TIMELOCK/ADMIN-protected functions because GovernanceTimelock.checkRole also returns a boolean without enforcing it, while Configurator ignores that result. Therefore, no effective guard prevents direct calls, and the deployer-specific hypothesis is confirmed, although the actual vulnerability is not limited to the deployment account and permits arbitrary callers to modify protected configuration.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Do not grant DAO to the deployment EOA, or revoke that grant after initialization. Ensure privileged configurator operations can only be executed by the timelock/governance executor and are subject to the intended delay.

Boosted rewards can be applied retroactively

This accounting error allows a newly acquired or increased reward boost to be applied retroactively to an earlier staking period. Reward accrual stores a single user checkpoint, while the boost multiplier is evaluated when the interval is later settled rather than being tied to the boost state throughout each portion of that interval.

A user can create an uncheckpointed reward interval, obtain or increase an esLBR lock near the end of that interval, and then call getReward(), stake(), withdraw(), or another function that updates rewards. The calculation may apply the newly observed boost to rewards earned before the boost became active, resulting in excess reward payments and dilution of other stakers' rewards.

Affected Components: the stakerewardV2pool contract, specifically getBoost(), earned(), getReward(), and reward-update logic; and the esLBR boost and locking mechanism

QA Review: Upon reviewing the updateReward modifier, getBoost, earned, and esLBRBoost.getUserBoost implementations, I confirmed that the accounting error is exploitable. updateReward records only one userUpdatedAt timestamp and earned() computes the entire uncheckpointed reward-per-token delta using the boost returned at claim or another update time. After a user stakes, the user can call esLBRBoost.setLockStatus near the end of the accrual period without any corresponding checkpoint in StakingRewardsV2. getBoost then passes the old userUpdatedAt to getUserBoost, which observes the newly created lock. While the lock remains active, getUserBoost returns the full miningBoost, causing earned() to multiply rewards accrued before the lock by the newly obtained boost. Calling getReward, stake, or withdraw then permanently checkpoints this inflated amount. There is no callback, access-control relationship, or synchronization mechanism forcing staking rewards to be checkpointed before setLockStatus, and the boost contract does not retain historical lock states. Therefore, the implementation permits retroactive application of a boost to previously accrued rewards.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Checkpoint staking rewards before any boost or lock status change, or calculate rewards over discrete intervals using historical boost state. Do not apply a newly observed boost to reward time preceding its activation.

medium

Protocol reward distributions are permanently stranded when no esLBR holders are staked

This accounting error can permanently strand protocol reward distributions when no esLBR holders are staked. The configurator transfers the reward amount to the protocol rewards pool before the pool verifies whether any tokens are staked. If totalStaked() is zero, notifyRewardAmount() returns without updating reward-per-token accounting or recording the transferred amount as pending rewards.

An actor can trigger a reward distribution while the pool has no staked esLBR and the configurator holds the required reward balance. The transferred tokens then remain in the rewards pool without being allocated to future stakers, causing a permanent loss of protocol rewards unless an administrative recovery mechanism exists.

Affected Components: the LybraConfigurator contract, specifically distributeRewards(); the ProtocolRewardsPool contract, specifically notifyRewardAmount(); and the EUSD vault repayment flow that triggers reward distribution

QA Review: Upon reviewing Configurator.distributeRewards and ProtocolRewardsPool.notifyRewardAmount, I confirmed the accounting vulnerability exists. distributeRewards transfers the thresholded peUSD or eUSD balance to the rewards pool before invoking notifyRewardAmount, without checking whether esLBR.totalSupply() is nonzero and without validating that notification succeeded. In notifyRewardAmount, the require(msg.sender == address(configurator)) check passes, but the function immediately returns when totalStaked() == 0, leaving rewardPerTokenStored and any pending-reward accounting unchanged. Because the transfer has already occurred, the tokens remain in ProtocolRewardsPool but are not assigned to current or future stakers through the reward-per-token mechanism. distributeRewards is externally callable, so an actor can invoke it whenever the configurator holds a threshold balance while no esLBR is staked; any protocol path that invokes it has the same effect. The rewards pool has no visible recovery or pending-distribution mechanism, and later notifications account only for newly notified amounts. Thus, the funds are effectively stranded from the intended distribution accounting, although they remain held by the pool.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Check totalStaked before transferring, or record pending rewards when there are no stakers and distribute them on the next valid notification. Verify transfer amounts and maintain explicit liabilities for undistributed rewards.

medium

LBR oracle result is used without zero, sign, or freshness validation during reward buyout

This oracle validation vulnerability allows an LBR price returned by the oracle to be used without verifying that it is positive, complete, fresh, and otherwise within an acceptable range. The reward buyout logic reads latestRoundData() but does not validate the round identifiers, updatedAt timestamp, answeredInRound value, or the signed price before casting it to uint256.

If the feed returns zero, stale, incomplete, negative, or otherwise invalid data, an eligible user can invoke purchaseOtherEarnings(..., true). A zero or invalid price may allow rewards to be purchased for no EUSD fee, while a negative or malformed value may cause the transaction to revert or produce unsafe calculations. This can result in incorrect reward pricing and disruption of the buyout mechanism.

Affected Components: the EUSDMiningIncentives contract, specifically purchaseOtherEarnings(); the LBR price oracle; and the esLBR token and reward buyout flow

QA Review: Upon reviewing the oracle-dependent functions in EUSDMiningIncentives.sol, I confirmed the reported oracle-validation weakness is exploitable. In purchaseOtherEarnings, the LBR feed result from latestRoundData() is destructured while roundId, updatedAt, answeredInRound, and validity of the signed answer are ignored. The code directly converts lbrPrice to uint256 and calculates the EUSD bidding fee without checking that the answer is positive, nonzero, complete, or fresh. A zero answer therefore reduces the fee to zero, allowing an otherwise eligible user to receive esLBR rewards without paying the intended EUSD cost. Negative answers can produce an invalid large unsigned value and commonly cause arithmetic overflow or transfer failure, creating a denial-of-service or reverted buyout path. Additionally, stakedLBRLpValue, used by isOtherEarningsClaimable, independently consumes the same unvalidated LBR oracle result, so zero or malformed data can also affect eligibility or revert. The isEUSDBuyoutAllowed flag only enables or disables the entire payment mode and does not validate oracle data.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require a positive answer, valid round metadata, and an acceptable updatedAt age before conversion. Define a bounded price sanity range and revert or disable the buyout when the feed is invalid.

StETH rebasing can make recorded collateral exceed the vault’s actual collateral

This collateral accounting error can cause the vault's recorded stETH collateral to exceed the amount of stETH it can actually redeem. If the vault tracks deposited token amounts without accounting for stETH's rebasing behavior, an adverse rebase can reduce the collateral's current redeemable value while the recorded balance remains unchanged.

A borrower can deposit stETH and borrow up to the permitted limit before an adverse rebase, then withdraw or abandon the position. The vault may continue to treat the original collateral amount as available even though its actual backing has decreased, leaving liquidators unable to recover the outstanding debt and creating undercollateralized positions. The same rebasing-aware valuation must be used consistently for borrowing limits, collateral ratios, withdrawals, and liquidations.

Affected Components: the stETH vault, specifically deposit() and depositEtherToStETH(); the EUSD vault base collateral-ratio, withdrawal, and liquidation logic; and the collateral token accounting used by borrowers and liquidators

QA Review: Upon reviewing LybraStETHDepositVault and LybraEUSDVaultBase, I confirmed the accounting mismatch is present and can become exploitable after an adverse stETH rebase. In depositEtherToMint, the vault records msg.value in both totalDepositedAsset and depositedAsset[msg.sender], even though Lido returns sharesAmount and the vault’s actual stETH balance is subject to rebasing. Similarly, depositAssetToMint records the nominal token amount rather than a share-based entitlement. The collateral-ratio checks in _checkHealth, liquidation, superLiquidation, rigidRedemption, and _withdraw all use depositedAsset, while getAssetPrice returns only the ETH oracle price in the stETH vault. Consequently, a negative rebase reduces collateralAsset.balanceOf(address(this)) without reducing recorded collateral or debt capacity. Withdrawals and liquidation may then attempt to transfer more stETH than the vault possesses and revert, leaving undercollateralized debt effectively unrecoverable. The positive-rebase excessIncomeDistribution path does not correct negative rebases. This is therefore a genuine accounting and insolvency risk, although exploitation depends on an adverse rebase occurring.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Track stETH shares or compute each position’s current redeemable stETH amount using a rebasing-aware representation. Use the same current-value calculation for borrowing limits, collateral ratios, withdrawals, and liquidation, and add tests covering negative and positive rebases.

Reward duration accepts zero and reward scheduling truncates emissions

This staking-reward logic error permits the reward duration to be set to zero and uses integer division without handling remainders. Because notifyRewardAmount() divides the scheduled reward amount by the configured duration, a zero duration causes subsequent reward notifications to revert due to division by zero, disrupting reward scheduling.

For non-divisible reward amounts, integer truncation also causes the calculated reward rate to be lower than the intended distribution. The residual rewards are not explicitly tracked or returned, leaving part of the scheduled allocation permanently undistributed. An owner or incorrectly configured governance executor could therefore cause a denial of service or repeated reward shortfalls.

Affected Components: the stakerewardV2pool contract, specifically setRewardsDuration() and notifyRewardAmount()

QA Review: Upon reviewing the StakingRewardsV2 contract, I confirmed the reported issue in setRewardsDuration and notifyRewardAmount. setRewardsDuration accepts any uint256 value, including zero, and only checks that the prior reward period has ended; it does not enforce a positive or minimum duration. Once the owner sets duration to zero, a later notifyRewardAmount call reaches either _amount / duration or (_amount + remainingRewards) / duration, causing a Solidity division-by-zero panic before the reward-ratio validation and preventing new rewards from being scheduled. The onlyOwner modifier limits this to the owner or compromised/misconfigured governance, but it does not eliminate the operational denial-of-service risk. Additionally, both reward-rate calculations use integer division, so amounts that are not evenly divisible by duration are truncated. The resulting rewardRatio multiplied by duration can be less than the scheduled amount, with no remainder tracking or refund mechanism; sufficiently small amounts can produce rewardRatio == 0 and revert. Therefore, the hypothesis is supported by the implementation.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require duration > 0 and enforce an appropriate minimum duration. Track and explicitly handle division remainders, or use fixed-point accounting so the full scheduled amount is either distributed or deliberately returned.

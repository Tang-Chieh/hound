Executive Summary

The Hound security team conducted this comprehensive audit of the ParaSpace protocol, reviewing its principal contracts, libraries, interfaces, oracle integrations, tokenization components, and external protocol dependencies.

The review covered the high-level ParaSpace system architecture, including user-facing pool and gateway entry points, shared pool and reserve state, protocol logic libraries, access-control and address-provider resolution, price-oracle flows, NFT and Uniswap position integrations, ApeCoin staking, and marketplace interactions.

Our team identified two confirmed high-severity issues: privileged NFT floor-price updates can bypass validation, and supply accounting can overmint shares for supported fee-on-transfer or deflationary assets. Two additional Uniswap-related concerns remain proposed and require implementation-level confirmation, while a separate Uniswap spot-price manipulation concern was rejected based on the available code context. The protocol’s security posture depends significantly on privileged administration, asset-listing controls, oracle integrity, and careful handling of external token and position-manager interactions.

System Overview

The ParaSpace architecture is centered on PoolCore, which implements and exposes IPool functionality for supplying assets, borrowing, withdrawing, liquidating positions, and coordinating collateral operations. PoolParameters provides configuration and administrative behavior, while PoolStorage exposes the fixed storage layout used by the pool. PoolCore and the surrounding logic libraries operate on shared DataTypes.PoolStorage state, with ReserveLogic, SupplyLogic, and LiquidationLogic handling reserve accounting, asset flows, and liquidation-related processing.

Protocol execution is divided across reusable libraries. SupplyLogic coordinates ERC20 transfers, reserve updates, and pToken minting and burning, while ReserveLogic maintains liquidity and reserve parameters. LiquidationLogic reads pool state to determine collateral and debt outcomes. The architecture therefore relies on consistent accounting between requested transfer amounts, actual token balances, reserve liquidity, and tokenized claims; deviations in any of these relationships can affect solvency and withdrawal availability.

Address and authorization dependencies are resolved through IPoolAddressesProvider and IACLManager. PoolCore uses the configured provider to locate protocol components and the price oracle, while privileged administration controls important configuration and oracle behavior. NFTFloorOracle supplies NFT pricing through IParaSpaceOracle, with ERC721OracleWrapper exposing the resulting values to consumers. The review identified that administrator-directed NFT price updates can bypass normal sanity, deviation, and feeder-agreement checks, allowing a compromised or misused administrator to publish inflated collateral prices that affect borrowing and liquidation decisions.

The system also includes specialized collateral integrations. NTokenUniswapV3 interacts with INonfungiblePositionManager and depends on pool interfaces and oracle-derived asset prices to value and manage Uniswap positions. NTokenApeStaking uses ApeStakingLogic to interact with ApeCoinStaking. These integrations cross trust boundaries and introduce external callback, pricing, and execution considerations. Available evidence did not establish that direct Uniswap spot-price manipulation inflates the principal position valuation, but potential reentrancy and zero-slippage unwind concerns remain subject to confirmation against the complete implementation.

WETHGateway and related gateway interfaces provide user-facing convenience flows that transfer WETH and delegate to IPool operations, while marketplace and wrapped-Punk integrations extend the protocol’s external interaction surface. Security depends on strict validation at these boundaries, correct authorization of nToken and pToken operations, robust oracle freshness and deviation controls, and asset-listing restrictions. In particular, SupplyLogic should either reject fee-on-transfer assets or account for the actual balance increase after transferFrom; otherwise, nominal supply amounts may exceed the underlying assets received by the reserve.

Administrator Can Bypass NFT Price Validation

This oracle-manipulation vulnerability results from privileged administrator price updates bypassing the validation constraints applied to ordinary oracle updates. In particular, administrator-controlled NFT floor prices may not be subject to equivalent nonzero, bounded, deviation, and freshness checks, allowing an invalid value to be accepted by the oracle and propagated to the protocol's collateral valuation logic.

An attacker who compromises or abuses the administrator role could submit an artificially high NFT floor price, causing PoolCore to overvalue NFT-backed collateral. The attacker could then borrow more assets than the collateral legitimately supports or avoid liquidation until the manipulated price is corrected, potentially creating bad debt and losses for the protocol. Administrator updates should undergo the same validation as other updates, with exceptional changes protected by timelocked or multisignature governance and accompanied by monitoring events.

Affected Components: the NFTFloorOracle contract, the price oracle interface, and the PoolCore contract, specifically its collateral valuation that consumes NFT floor prices

QA Review: Upon reviewing NFTFloorOracle.setPrice and its supporting validation logic, I confirmed that administrator updates bypass the oracle’s price-sanity and aggregation protections. When the caller has DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE, setPrice immediately invokes _finalizePrice(_asset, _twap) and returns, without calling _checkValidity, requiring a nonzero value, enforcing MAX_DEVIATION_RATE, or requiring agreement from the minimum three feeders. The same behavior applies through setMultiplePrices, which repeatedly calls setPrice. _finalizePrice records the arbitrary value as the current twap and refreshes both block and timestamp, so the resulting price also appears fresh to consumers. ERC721OracleWrapper.latestAnswer and getAnswer return this value through NFTFloorOracle.getPrice, while PoolCore passes the configured price oracle into borrowing, withdrawal, liquidation, and auction logic, allowing collateral valuation to be affected. Although the path requires compromise or misuse of the privileged administrator role, the code clearly permits an administrator to inject an inflated or zero NFT price without the stated validation constraints. Therefore, the hypothesis is a real privileged oracle-manipulation vulnerability.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Require administrator updates to pass the same validation as all other updates, including nonzero and bounded prices, maximum deviation from the previous value, and freshness constraints. Use a timelocked or multisignature governance path for exceptional changes, and emit update events suitable for monitoring.

Supply Accounting May Overmint Shares for Fee-on-Transfer ERC20 Assets

This accounting error occurs because the supply flow may credit the reserve and mint pTokens based on the amount requested for transfer rather than the amount actually received. If a supported ERC20 token applies transfer fees or otherwise delivers fewer tokens than requested, SupplyLogic can record an inflated reserve contribution and issue shares that are not fully backed by underlying assets.

An attacker could supply a fee-on-transfer or deflationary token, receive shares calculated from the nominal transfer amount, and later redeem those shares for more tokens than were actually deposited. This can dilute other suppliers, distort liquidity and interest-rate calculations, and create an undercollateralized reserve. The protocol should either restrict supported assets to standard ERC20 tokens with exact-transfer behavior or calculate the balance difference before and after transferFrom and use the actual received amount consistently for accounting and share minting.

Affected Components: the SupplyLogic contract, the PoolCore contract, the PoolStorageData contract, and the pToken interface, specifically the ERC20 supply, reserve accounting, and pToken minting flow

QA Review: Upon reviewing PoolCore.supply and SupplyLogic.executeSupply (approximately lines 73-119), I confirmed the reported accounting vulnerability. The function validates and updates reserve interest rates using params.amount, calls IERC20(params.asset).safeTransferFrom for that nominal amount, and then mints the same params.amount of pTokens through IPToken.mint. No reserve balance is measured before and after the transfer, and GPv2SafeERC20 only verifies transfer-call success; it does not verify the amount actually received. Consequently, a listed fee-on-transfer or deflationary token can cause the xToken reserve to receive less underlying than the amount represented by minted shares. The subsequent withdraw path, SupplyLogic.executeWithdraw, burns pTokens and instructs the xToken to transfer the nominal underlying amount, potentially consuming pre-existing reserve liquidity or causing withdrawals to fail once liquidity is insufficient. The shown validation and reserve configuration code does not impose a transfer-behavior check or reject such assets. The issue is therefore exploitable when governance lists a non-standard token, although the exact loss magnitude also depends on the xToken implementation and available reserve liquidity.
Suggested Mitigation (not independently verified): Either restrict reserve listings to standard ERC20s with no transfer fees, or measure the reserve balance before and after transferFrom and use the actual received amount consistently for reserve accounting, interest-rate updates, and share minting. Reject transfers whose received amount does not match the expected amount if exact-amount semantics are required.
